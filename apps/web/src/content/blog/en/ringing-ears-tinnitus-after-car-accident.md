The crash is over, but the noise is not. There is a high-pitched ringing in one ear, or a buzzing that gets louder at night when the house is quiet. Sounds feel muffled, as if you had water in your ear.

Ringing in the ears — **tinnitus** — is common after a crash, and when it comes from a loud noise it may fade over time. But it can also be the first sign of an injury to the ear, a concussion or a sudden hearing loss that needs treatment quickly. Here is how to tell the difference.

In the small study of airbag ear injuries, hearing loss was also found in 85% of the patients, half had balance problems, and having the ear turned toward the airbag was linked to hearing loss and eardrum perforation. It is a small group of people who went to an ear clinic, so it does not tell how often this happens in all crashes, but it shows the injury is real and can last.

NIDCD explains that a loud noise can cause a temporary hearing loss that disappears 16 to 48 hours later, and that tinnitus from noise may subside over time — but even when hearing seems to come back, there may be lasting damage. That is why a ringing that "went away" is still worth mentioning.

## When to call your provider

MedlinePlus recommends contacting a health care provider if:

- The ear noises started after a head injury.
- They come with other symptoms, such as dizziness, feeling off balance, nausea or vomiting.
- The noise is only in one ear and continues for several weeks or longer.
- The noise is pulsing, with a steady beat.
- It still bothers you after trying self-care.

After a crash, the first two apply to almost everyone, so it is worth mentioning the ringing at your first visit even if it seems minor.

## What the evaluation looks like

A provider will look in your ear, ask about the crash and the airbag, check your neck, jaw and balance, and screen for signs of concussion. Depending on what they find, you may be referred to an audiologist for a hearing test or to an ear, nose and throat (ENT) specialist. NIDCD notes that imaging is usually ordered when the tinnitus pulses.

## What helps while it settles

There is no pill that cures tinnitus. NIDCD says no medication is approved specifically for it and that no vitamins or supplements have been proven to work. What does help:

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>Things you can do now</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Protect your ears from loud noise: concerts, power tools and loud headphones can make it worse.</li>
    <li>Use low background sound at night — a fan, soft music or a sound app — so the silence does not amplify the ringing.</li>
    <li>Treat what comes with it: neck pain, jaw pain and poor sleep often make tinnitus feel louder. See <a href="/en/blog/trouble-sleeping-after-car-accident">trouble sleeping after a car accident</a>.</li>
    <li>Write down when it started, which ear, how loud it is and what makes it better or worse.</li>
    <li>If it lasts, ask about sound therapy, hearing aids when there is hearing loss, and cognitive behavioral therapy, which NIDCD lists among the options.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## The short version

Ringing in the ears after a crash may fade over time, but it can come from an airbag blast, a torn eardrum, a concussion or a neck or jaw injury. Sudden hearing loss in one ear is an emergency, and so is ringing with vomiting, confusion or a worsening headache. Mention the ringing at your first visit, protect your ears and get a hearing evaluation if it does not go away.
