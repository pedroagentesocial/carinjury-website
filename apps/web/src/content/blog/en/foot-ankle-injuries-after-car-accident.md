You saw the other car coming and pressed the brake as hard as you could. In the crash, your foot was pinned against the pedal and the floor pushed up toward you. Now your ankle is swollen, your heel hurts when you step and you are not sure if it is a sprain or something more.

Foot and ankle injuries are easy to overlook after a crash, especially when your neck or back hurt more at first. But **the feet take a lot of force in a frontal crash**: the driver's foot is often braced on the pedal, and the footwell can be pushed in. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS), car crashes are one of the main causes of heel bone fractures.

## How the crash injures the foot and ankle

- **Braking at the moment of impact.** The foot is locked against the pedal, so the force travels through the ankle and the heel.
- **Footwell intrusion.** In a strong frontal crash, the floor and pedals can be pushed toward the driver and trap the foot.
- **Twisting.** The foot can roll or twist under the pedal or against the floor, spraining or breaking the ankle.
- **Getting out of the car.** Stepping out quickly onto uneven ground after a crash is another common way to roll an ankle.

The AAOS notes that a severe sprain can look like a broken bone and needs prompt evaluation. It also points out that a high-energy heel fracture can come with other injuries, such as fractures of the spine, hip or the other heel. That is why a strong crash calls for a complete evaluation, not just a look at the foot. If your knee or hip also hurt, see [knee pain after a car accident](/en/blog/knee-pain-after-car-accident) and [hip pain after a car accident](/en/blog/hip-pain-after-car-accident).

## How it is evaluated

The provider checks where it hurts, how much swelling there is, whether you can bear weight and how stable the ankle is. They also check circulation and sensation in the foot. Depending on the findings, they may order X-rays to look for fractures, and CT or MRI for complex fractures or ligament injuries. Read more in [X-ray, MRI or CT after a car accident](/en/blog/xray-mri-ct-after-car-accident).

## Treatment and recovery

Fractures that are out of place usually need surgery, and a heel fracture often means no weight on that heel for about three months. For sprains and stable fractures, treatment is usually conservative:

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>What usually helps</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Rest and protection: a brace, boot or cast, as your provider directs.</li>
    <li>Ice for 20 to 30 minutes, 3 or 4 times a day, wrapped in a towel (AAOS).</li>
    <li>Compression and elevating the ankle above the level of your heart, especially in the first 48 hours.</li>
    <li>Physical therapy for range of motion, strength and balance once the early phase passes.</li>
    <li>Returning gradually to walking, stairs and work, without skipping steps.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

Balance exercises matter: after an ankle injury, the joint can feel unstable for a while, and rehab helps prevent another sprain. See [your first six weeks of rehab after a crash](/en/blog/first-six-weeks-physical-therapy-after-crash).

## Driving and work

Ask your provider when it is safe to drive. With a boot or cast on the right foot, you may not be able to brake quickly enough. If your job keeps you on your feet, ask for written work restrictions: read [going back to work after an accident](/en/blog/back-to-work-lost-wages-car-accident-utah).

## The short version

A foot braced on the brake takes a lot of force in a crash. Bone through the skin, a deformed ankle, a numb or cold foot, or not being able to bear weight need the ER. For everything else: get evaluated, protect the ankle, follow the rehab plan and do not drive until your provider says your foot is ready.
