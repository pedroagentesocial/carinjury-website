At first you thought it was just a bruise. But two days later the knee is swollen, going down stairs feels risky, and you feel like it could give out at any moment.

In a frontal crash, your knees are the first thing the dashboard finds. And because attention tends to go to the neck and back, **knee injuries are easy to miss.**

## How a crash hurts the knee

When the car stops suddenly, your body keeps moving forward and the bent knee hits the dashboard. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS), that blow pushes the shinbone backward relative to the thigh bone and is a classic cause of **posterior cruciate ligament (PCL)** injury. The same impact can break the kneecap.

If you were also braking hard or your leg got twisted in the footwell, the meniscus and other ligaments can suffer too. And the force does not always stop there: it can travel up to the hip, so tell the examiner if that area hurts as well.

<div class="pa-note pa-note-alert">
  <span class="pa-note-icon">🚨</span>
  <div>
    <p><strong>Go to the ER right away if you have any of these signs:</strong></p>
    <p>Visible deformity or a kneecap out of place · you cannot put weight on the foot or walk · you cannot straighten the knee or do a straight leg raise · a cold, pale or numb foot · an open wound over the knee · fast, severe swelling · calf pain and swelling, or shortness of breath in the days after the crash.</p>

## The most common injuries

<div class="pa-table-wrap">
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th>Injury</th><th>How it feels</th><th>How it is confirmed</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Contusion</td><td>Bruising and pain at the front of the knee that improve over days to a few weeks</td><td>Physical exam</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Posterior cruciate ligament (PCL)</td><td>Pain and swelling that build quickly, stiffness, a limp, a sense the knee will give way</td><td>Exam; MRI</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Kneecap fracture</td><td>Pain and swelling at the front, bruising, you cannot straighten the knee or keep the leg straight</td><td>X-ray</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Meniscus tear</td><td>A "pop," stiffness and swelling that grow over 2 to 3 days, the knee locks or catches</td><td>Exam and MRI</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

An **X-ray** rules out fractures but does not show ligaments or menisci: that is what an **MRI** is for. So a normal X-ray is not always the end of the story if the knee stays swollen or unstable. We explain it in [X-ray, CT or MRI after a car accident](/en/blog/xray-mri-ct-after-car-accident).

## What to do in the first days

If you have no red flags, and until your evaluation:

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>Early knee care</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Rest from what hurts. If you are limping, crutches help take weight off.</li>
    <li>Apply cold for 20 minutes, several times a day, without putting ice directly on the skin.</li>
    <li>Use an elastic bandage to control swelling.</li>
    <li>Elevate the leg above heart level when you can.</li>
    <li>Photograph the bruise and note which movements are hard: stairs, bending, driving.</li>
    <li>Get evaluated even if the pain seems minor: if the knee locks or gives way, do not wait for it to pass.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## What recovery looks like

It depends on the injury, but many are treated without surgery, especially at first.

<div class="pa-timeline">
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Phase 1</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Reduce swelling and protect</p>
    <p>Relative rest, cold, compression and elevation. With a PCL injury, a brace that keeps the shinbone from sagging backward is sometimes used, along with crutches.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Phase 2</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Regain motion</p>
    <p>Physical therapy to bend and straighten the knee without pain and walk normally again.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Phase 3</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Regain strength</p>
    <p>Strengthening, especially the quadriceps, is what gives the knee back its stability.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">If it does not improve</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Orthopedic referral</p>
    <p>Displaced fractures, a meniscus that locks the knee, or instability that does not settle may need an orthopedic surgeon's opinion.</p>
  </div>
</div>

To see which part of treatment does the real work, read [what the evidence says about passive and active care](/en/blog/passive-vs-active-care-after-car-accident).

## Why early documentation matters

A knee that first shows up in your chart weeks later is easier for an insurer to dispute. Mention the knee pain from your first evaluation, even if your neck hurts more. Those gaps carry weight, as we explain in [how gaps in treatment hurt your claim](/en/blog/gaps-in-treatment-hurt-your-claim).

## The short version

If your knee hit the dashboard, do not settle for "it's a bruise." Swelling that grows, a sense it will give way or being unable to straighten it are signs of a real injury. Get evaluated early and mention the knee from your first appointment.
