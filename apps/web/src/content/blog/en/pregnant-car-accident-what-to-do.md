It was a light tap in a parking lot. The car barely has a mark, you feel fine and the baby is moving. The natural thing is to assume you do not need a doctor.

**During pregnancy, that is exactly the call to avoid.** After any crash, however small it seems, call your doctor or go to the ER the same day.

## Why a minor crash matters more in pregnancy

The placenta is attached to the wall of the uterus, and the two respond differently to a blow or a sudden stop. That force can separate them partly or completely: this is a **placental abruption**, and it can cut off the baby's oxygen.

The tricky part is that it does not always show right away. According to a review published by the American Academy of Family Physicians (AAFP), about 9 in 10 traumas during pregnancy are minor, and yet minor injuries account for 60 to 70% of trauma-related fetal losses. An abruption can occur up to 24 hours after the injury.

So feeling fine does not rule out the problem. Neither does a car with barely any damage, as we explain in [low-speed crash injuries](/en/blog/low-speed-crash-injuries).

It depends on how many weeks along you are and how the crash happened, but it usually includes:

If you do not know your blood type, do not worry: they check it at the evaluation. Bring your prenatal care information if you have it handy.

## After you are discharged

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>What to do in the days that follow</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Tell your OB-GYN about the crash, even if you were already seen in the ER.</li>
    <li>Watch the baby's movements and follow your doctor's instructions.</li>
    <li>Go back right away if any warning sign appears.</li>
    <li>Tell every provider who treats you that you are pregnant, before any X-ray, medication or treatment.</li>
    <li>Keep your medical reports and bills: they are part of your claim.</li>
    <li>Do not give recorded statements or sign a release with the insurer before you know your diagnosis.</li>
  </ul>

## How to wear your seat belt correctly

Wearing a seat belt during pregnancy protects you both. What matters is the position:

- **Lap belt:** low, under your belly, across your hips. Never over the bump.
- **Shoulder belt:** between your breasts and beside your belly, across the middle of your collarbone. Never under your arm or behind your back.
- **Air bags:** leave them on. They reduce injuries and do not raise the risk of pregnancy complications.

## The short version

If you are pregnant and had a crash, even a minor one and even if you feel fine, get medical care the same day. A placental abruption may give no signs at first, and those hours of monitoring are what protect your baby.
