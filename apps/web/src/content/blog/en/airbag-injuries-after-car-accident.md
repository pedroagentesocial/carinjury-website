The airbag went off, the car filled with a white powder and your ears were ringing. Now your forearms look like they have a rug burn, your chest is sore and one eye feels gritty.

Airbags are one of the most important safety features in your car. According to figures from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), airbags saved more than **50,000 lives** between 1987 and 2017. But to protect you, they inflate in a fraction of a second, and that force can cause injuries of its own. Most are minor, but some need care right away.

## How an airbag injures

When sensors detect a strong enough crash, a chemical reaction fills the bag with gas almost instantly. That leaves three ways to get hurt:

- **The impact itself.** The bag comes out of the steering wheel or dashboard at high speed. If you sit too close, it hits your face, chest or arms instead of cushioning them.
- **Heat and friction.** The surface of the bag and the hot gas can scrape and burn the skin, especially on the forearms, hands, chest and face.
- **The powder and gases.** The dust released when the bag opens can irritate your eyes, skin and airways, and it can trigger an asthma attack in people who have asthma.

## Why airbag burns deserve a prompt checkup

Airbag burns are easy to dismiss as "just a scrape," and some get worse over the following days. In a small pilot study of 14 patients treated by a plastic surgeon for airbag injuries, **only 6 saw a doctor within the first month**, and 9 still had ongoing problems at follow-up, most often skin discoloration. Patients seen within 30 days showed more improvement in skin color and healing than those who waited. It is a small study, but the message is clear: do not wait to have a burn checked.

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>What to do in the first hours</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Rinse powder off your skin with cool water and mild soap, and change your clothes.</li>
    <li>If powder got into your eyes, rinse them with clean water for several minutes and do not rub them. Remove contact lenses.</li>
    <li>Cover burns and scrapes with a clean, non-stick dressing. Do not pop blisters.</li>
    <li>Take photos of every mark on the same day, and again over the next few days as they change.</li>
    <li>Get evaluated even if you feel fine: chest, neck and wrist injuries are not always obvious at first.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## How to lower the risk next time

- **Sit back.** NHTSA recommends at least 10 inches between the center of your breastbone and the center of the steering wheel.
- **Tilt the wheel toward your chest,** not toward your face, if it adjusts.
- **Keep your hands on the sides of the wheel,** not across the center where the airbag comes out.
- **Children 12 and under ride in the back seat,** and a rear-facing car seat should never go in front of an active airbag.
- **Always wear the seatbelt.** Airbags are designed to work together with it, not to replace it.

## The short version

Airbags save lives, but they can burn, scrape and bruise, and they can hurt your eyes, wrists and chest. Eye pain or vision changes, trouble breathing and large or blistered burns need the ER. For everything else: rinse the powder off, photograph the marks, and get a full evaluation in the first few days instead of waiting to see if it heals on its own.
