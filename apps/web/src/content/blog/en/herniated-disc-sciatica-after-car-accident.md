Back pain after a crash is common. Back pain that **travels down one leg** is a different conversation.

When pain, tingling or weakness follows a defined path from the low back into the buttock, thigh, calf and sometimes the foot, it usually means a nerve root is being irritated or compressed — most often by a disc that was pushed out of position by the collision.

The headline is worth sitting with: **most disc herniations improve without surgery**, but the improvement is measured in months, not days. Patients who expect resolution in two weeks tend to abandon the exact treatment that was working.

## How a crash injures a disc that "looked fine before"

The most common objection from an insurer is that the disc was already degenerating. Often it was — after age 30, imaging finds disc changes in a large share of people with no symptoms at all.

The clinical question is not whether degeneration existed. It is whether it was **symptomatic** before the crash. A disc that was quietly ageing and a disc that suddenly compresses a nerve root are different clinical situations, and the medical record — first symptom date, first exam, matching nerve findings — is what distinguishes them.

<div class="pa-note">
  <span class="pa-note-icon">🧭</span>
  <div>
    <p><strong>Match the map, not the picture.</strong> An MRI that shows a herniation at L5-S1 only supports your case if your symptoms and exam findings follow the S1 nerve path — calf and outer foot pain, reduced ankle reflex, weakness pushing the foot down. Imaging findings that do not line up with the exam are the ones adjusters dismantle most easily. ## The conservative treatment sequence

<div class="pa-timeline">
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Weeks 0–2</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Calm the nerve down</p>
    <p>Pain control, activity modification, positions that centralise symptoms toward the spine rather than down the leg. Avoid prolonged bed rest — it delays recovery.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Weeks 2–6</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Directional exercise and manual care</p>
    <p>Specific, repeated movements that reduce leg symptoms; traction or flexion-distraction where indicated; graded core and hip work. This is the window where most measurable gains happen.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Weeks 6–12</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Load and endurance</p>
    <p>Strengthening the deep stabilisers, hips and posterior chain so the segment tolerates real life: lifting, sitting, driving.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">If progress stalls</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Escalate deliberately</p>
    <p>Imaging correlated with the exam, then epidural steroid injection or a surgical consult. Surgery tends to relieve leg pain faster; long-term outcomes converge with conservative care in most patients.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="pa-note pa-note-alert">
  <span class="pa-note-icon">🚨</span>
  <div>
    <p><strong>Emergency signs — go to an ER now, do not wait for an appointment:</strong> loss of bladder or bowel control · numbness in the inner thighs, groin or buttocks (saddle anaesthesia) · rapidly worsening weakness in one or both legs · fever with severe back pain. These can indicate cauda equina syndrome, which is time-critical.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>What helps your recovery — and your record</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Report the exact path of the pain, not just "my back hurts". Draw it on a body chart.</li>
    <li>Note weakness precisely: which movement, which side, what you can no longer lift or push.</li>
    <li>Keep walking. Short, frequent walks beat both rest and heavy exercise.</li>
    <li>Change position every 30 minutes; sustained sitting loads the lumbar discs most.</li>
    <li>Do the home exercises daily — twice a week in clinic is not the treatment, it is the coaching.</li>
    <li>Do not stop when the leg pain fades. Centralisation is progress, not discharge.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## The short version

Leg symptoms after a crash mean a nerve is involved, and that changes both the diagnosis and the plan. The research is genuinely encouraging — roughly 80% show notable improvement by three months without surgery — but that number belongs to people who stayed in consistent, progressive treatment. The ones who stop at week three are not in it.
