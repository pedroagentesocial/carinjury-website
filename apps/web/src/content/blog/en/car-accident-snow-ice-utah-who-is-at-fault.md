The light turned yellow, you pressed the brake and nothing happened. The car kept sliding on a road that looked wet but was ice, and you ended up in the back of the car in front of you. Or someone else slid into you, got out and said, *"Sorry, it's the ice, nobody's fault."*

Many people think a winter crash is "an act of nature" and that nobody is responsible. **In Utah, that is usually not how it works. The law asks every driver to adjust to the conditions, so snow and ice rarely excuse a crash; they raise the standard of care.** Here is what that means for your claim.

## What Utah law expects from drivers in winter

Two sections of the Utah traffic code decide most winter crash claims:

- **Speed for the conditions (Utah Code 41-6a-601).** A driver may not go faster than is "reasonable and prudent under the existing conditions," with regard to the hazards that exist, including those due to **weather or highway conditions**. The same section also counts it as a problem when speed causes a driver to fail to keep control of the vehicle or stay in a single lane. In other words, you can be within the posted limit and still be going too fast for a snowy road.
- **Following distance (Utah Code 41-6a-711).** A driver may not follow another vehicle more closely than is reasonable and prudent, having regard for the speed, the traffic and **the condition of the highway**, and must leave at least two seconds behind the car ahead. On ice, two seconds is the floor, not the goal.

That is why "the road was icy" usually does not close a claim. The question becomes: *did each driver drive the way a careful person would on that road, that day?*

These are tendencies, not rules. Each insurer looks at the facts: speed, distance, the condition of the tires, whether the road had been plowed, what the police report says and what witnesses saw.

## If you were partly at fault, too

Utah uses comparative fault (Utah Code 78B-5-818). You can still claim against another driver if their fault is greater than yours, and your recovery is reduced by your own percentage. If your fault is equal to or greater than theirs, you cannot collect from them. In a pileup, fault is apportioned among everyone involved until it adds up to 100%. The details, with examples, are in [what if the accident was partly my fault](/en/blog/partly-at-fault-car-accident-utah).

Expect the adjuster to suggest you were going too fast for the snow too. Do not argue about it on a recorded call, and do not guess your speed. Read [what to say to the insurance adjuster](/en/blog/what-to-say-insurance-adjuster) first.

## Who pays your medical bills first

Fault decides who pays in the end, not who pays first. Utah is a no-fault state, and your own **Personal Injury Protection (PIP)** pays your first medical bills and part of your lost wages regardless of who caused the crash, with a minimum of $3,000 in medical coverage. After that, the claim goes against the at-fault driver's liability insurance, and if that driver has no insurance or drove off, your uninsured motorist coverage may apply. See [who pays my medical bills after a car accident in Utah](/en/blog/who-pays-medical-bills-car-accident-utah) and [hit by an uninsured driver in Utah](/en/blog/hit-by-uninsured-driver-utah).

If a snowplow or any other government vehicle was involved, or you believe a road defect caused the crash, a different and much shorter clock applies: a written notice of claim within one year under the Governmental Immunity Act of Utah. Read [Utah car accident deadlines](/en/blog/utah-car-accident-claim-deadlines) and talk to a lawyer early.

## Evidence that disappears with the snow

Winter evidence melts, gets plowed or gets salted within hours. While you wait for help, and only if it is safe:

- **Photos of the road surface** at the exact spot: ice, slush, packed snow, tire tracks and skid marks.
- **Photos of the other car's tires**, the damage on both cars and where each car came to rest.
- **The weather and the light:** snowfall, fog, whether the street lights were on.
- **Names and phone numbers of witnesses**, including drivers who stopped without sliding. They prove the road could be driven with care.
- **Nearby cameras:** businesses, doorbells, dash cams. Ask early; many recordings are overwritten in days.
- **The police report.** In Utah, a crash with an injury, or with $2,500 or more in apparent property damage, must be reported to police right away. See [your Utah crash report](/en/blog/utah-crash-report-how-to-get-it).

## The short version

Snow and ice do not erase fault in Utah. The law expects every driver to slow down for the conditions and to leave a safe distance for the condition of the road, so the driver who slid into you is usually still responsible, and your own share of fault, if any, is weighed under the 50% rule. Your PIP pays your first medical bills either way. Document the road before it changes, keep your words careful with the adjuster, and do not wait for the soreness to go away on its own. For the first hours after any crash, read [what to do in the first 24 hours](/en/blog/what-to-do-first-24-hours-after-accident).
