At the scene, a cut on your hand or your forehead seems like the least of your problems. The bleeding slows, someone hands you a napkin, and by the time the tow truck leaves you have almost forgotten it. But a crash is a dirty place to get cut: glass, torn metal, plastic trim and whatever was on the road.

**Most small cuts heal fine with good first aid. The ones that cause trouble are the deep ones that needed stitches, the ones with something still inside, and the dirty ones that needed a tetanus shot nobody thought about.** Here is how to sort them out.

## First aid for a cut or a puncture

MedlinePlus recommends the same basics for minor wounds:

- **Wash your hands first,** then wash the cut gently with mild soap and water.
- **Press directly on it** with a clean cloth to stop the bleeding.
- **Cover it.** Apply an antibacterial ointment and a clean bandage that will not stick to the wound.
- **For a puncture,** rinse it under running water for 5 minutes, then wash it with soap. Look for anything inside, but do not dig around for it.

Glass deserves extra respect. A fragment you cannot see can stay in a cut or a puncture and keep it from healing. If you think something is still in there, get it checked rather than trying to remove it yourself.

## Does it need stitches?

According to MedlinePlus, contact a provider right away, because the wound may need stitches, if:

Do not wait days to decide. A cut that gapes open, keeps reopening when you move, or sits over a joint deserves an evaluation the same day.

## Tetanus: the shot people forget

Tetanus bacteria are found in the environment, and the CDC explains that they get into the body through broken skin, usually through injuries. Road grime, debris and torn metal make crash wounds a classic setup.

The usual rule is a tetanus booster every 10 years, and MedlinePlus says to seek care if you have not had one in the last 10 years. But the CDC is more specific for wounds: the booster is due **after 5 years in the case of a severe or dirty wound or burn.** If you cannot remember your last tetanus shot, assume it is time to ask.

## Caring for stitches

MedlinePlus gives these instructions after a laceration is closed with stitches or staples:

- **Keep the area clean and dry** for the first 24 to 48 hours (completely dry for staples).
- **After that,** gently wash around the site once or twice a day with cool water and soap, and pat it dry; do not scrub the stitches.
- **Change the bandage** as your provider instructed, and keep your activity low so the wound does not reopen.
- **Ask when to come back** to have the wound checked and the stitches removed. If nobody told you, call to set it up.

## Signs of infection

Call your provider if you notice any of these, from MedlinePlus:

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>Watch for</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Warmth and redness around the wound, or a red streak spreading from it.</li>
    <li>A painful, throbbing feeling, or pain that does not go away.</li>
    <li>Swelling, or pus-like drainage.</li>
    <li>A fever of 100°F or higher.</li>
    <li>New numbness or tingling, a wound that splits open, or stitches coming out too early.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## A cut is also part of your record

Cuts heal and scars fade, so photograph them early and again while they heal, and make sure every wound is written down in your chart, even small ones. ## The short version

Clean every cut, press to stop the bleeding and cover it. Get it checked the same day if it is deeper than a quarter inch, on your face, reaches the bone, or has glass or debris in it, and never pull out an object that is stuck. Ask about a tetanus shot: after a dirty wound, 5 years since your last one is enough to need a booster. Then watch for redness, warmth, pus or fever while it heals.
