You walked away. You told the officer you were fine. You went to work the next morning.

And then, somewhere between day two and day four, your neck locked up, the headaches started, and you could not turn your head to back out of the driveway.

This is not unusual — it is the rule, not the exception.

## Why your body lies to you for 48 hours

During impact, your nervous system floods with adrenaline and cortisol. That fight-or-flight response raises your pain threshold, tightens muscles and keeps you functional long enough to get out of the road. The CDC describes this same masking effect: right after a crash you can feel energetic and "okay" while tissue damage already exists.

Then two things happen at once:

1. **Adrenaline drops.** The chemical anaesthesia wears off, usually within a few hours to a day.
2. **Inflammation peaks.** Torn muscle fibres, stretched ligaments and irritated joints swell. Swelling is what actually produces stiffness, nerve pressure and pain — and it builds over 24 to 72 hours.

That is why the day after the crash is almost always worse than the day of the crash.

## The delayed-symptom timeline

<div class="pa-timeline">
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Hours 0–6</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">The "I'm fine" phase</p>
    <p>Adrenaline peaks. You may feel shaky or oddly calm. Pain is suppressed. This is when most people decline an ambulance and sign statements they later regret.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Hours 6–24</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">First stiffness</p>
    <p>Neck and upper-back tightness, a dull headache at the base of the skull, soreness across the seat-belt line. Sleep is often disrupted.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Days 1–3</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Peak inflammation</p>
    <p>Range of motion drops sharply. Headaches intensify. Tingling or numbness in the arms can appear as swelling irritates nerve roots. Most whiplash cases become obvious here.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Days 3–14</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Cognitive and nerve symptoms</p>
    <p>Concentration problems, memory lapses, irritability, dizziness, light sensitivity — signs of concussion that were invisible at the scene. Radiating leg pain may signal a disc injury.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Weeks 2–12</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">The fork in the road</p>
    <p>With active treatment, most people improve steadily. Without it, guarding and scar tissue set in and acute pain starts turning chronic.</p>
  </div>
</div>

  <span class="pa-note-icon">🚨</span>

    <p><strong>Go to an emergency room immediately — do not wait for a clinic appointment — if you have any of these:</strong></p>
    <p>Worsening or "worst ever" headache · repeated vomiting · one pupil larger than the other · confusion, slurred speech or unusual drowsiness · seizure · weakness or numbness in an arm or leg that is getting worse · abdominal pain, bruising across the belly, fainting or dizziness on standing (possible internal bleeding) · chest pain or trouble breathing.</p>

## The second reason waiting costs you

There is a medical cost to waiting — untreated soft tissue heals stiff and short. But there is also a documentation cost, and it is the one patients never see coming.

Insurance adjusters read the gap between the crash date and your first medical visit as evidence that the injury was not serious, or was not caused by the crash. A delay of a few days is explainable. A delay of a few weeks becomes the centrepiece of the denial letter. We cover exactly how this argument works in [gaps in treatment and how they hurt your claim](/en/blog/gaps-in-treatment-hurt-your-claim).

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>What to do in the 72 hours after a crash</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Get a baseline evaluation within 24–72 hours, even if you feel fine.</li>
    <li>Keep a short daily symptom log: date, symptom, intensity 0–10, what it stopped you doing.</li>
    <li>Photograph visible bruising — including the seat-belt line — as it develops.</li>
    <li>Tell the examiner about every symptom, including "minor" ones like poor sleep or fogginess.</li>
    <li>Avoid strenuous activity and prolonged bed rest. Gentle movement beats both.</li>
    <li>Do not give a recorded statement or sign a release before you know your diagnosis.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## The short version

Feeling fine at the scene tells you almost nothing. The honest picture of a crash injury appears somewhere between hour 24 and day 14, and the people who get evaluated early are the ones who recover fastest and have the least to argue about later.
