The morning after the crash you find them: a purple stripe where the seatbelt crossed your chest, a dark patch on your knee from the dashboard, a sore spot on your arm you do not remember hitting. Bruises are the most visible mark a crash leaves, and usually the least worrying.

Usually. **Most bruises are blood trapped under the skin and fade on their own, but where a bruise is and how it behaves can point to an injury underneath that needs attention.** Here is how to tell the difference.

## What a bruise is, and how it changes

A bruise is blood trapped under the skin after small blood vessels break. MedlinePlus lists car accidents among the most common causes. A typical bruise starts pinkish red and tender, turns blue or purple, then greenish-yellow, and finally fades back to your normal skin color. Most last about two weeks; some take months.

You are more likely to bruise, and to bruise more, if you take blood thinners such as warfarin or aspirin, or antiplatelet medicines such as clopidogrel. If that is you or an older relative, read [older adults after a car accident](/en/blog/older-adults-after-car-accident).

## Caring for an ordinary bruise

- **Ice it.** Wrap ice in a towel and apply it for up to 15 minutes each hour. Never put ice directly on the skin.
- **Raise it.** Keep the bruised area above the level of your heart when you can, so blood does not pool there.
- **Rest it.** Give the area a break from heavy use for a few days.
- **For pain,** MedlinePlus suggests acetaminophen if needed. Read [ibuprofen or Tylenol after a car accident](/en/blog/pain-relievers-after-car-accident) before reaching for the bottle.

## Compartment syndrome: rare, but time matters

Muscles in the arms and legs sit in compartments wrapped in a tough tissue called fascia, which does not stretch. MedlinePlus explains that after a crush injury, a broken bone, a very bruised muscle or a severe sprain, swelling can raise the pressure inside a compartment until it cuts off blood flow. It happens most often in the lower leg and the forearm.

The warning sign is **pain much greater than expected for the injury**, pain that does not go away with pain medicine or by raising the limb, plus numbness, tingling, weakness or pale skin. With an acute injury, symptoms can become severe within a few hours. It needs surgery right away: permanent nerve injury can occur after less than 12 to 24 hours of compression, and muscle can be damaged even faster. Caught early, the outlook is excellent.

## A bruise is also a record

Bruises fade, and with them part of the visible evidence of what the crash did to your body. Photograph them in the first days and again as they change, with something for scale and the date. Tell your provider about every bruised area, even ones that do not hurt much, so they end up in your chart. See [how to document your injuries for an insurance claim](/en/blog/how-to-document-injuries-insurance-claim). And remember that pain under a bruise can grow over the next days: read [delayed symptoms after a car accident](/en/blog/delayed-symptoms-after-car-accident).

## The short version

Bruises after a crash are common, and most fade in about two weeks with ice, elevation and rest. Pay attention to where they are and how they behave: a seatbelt bruise across the belly, bruising behind the ears or under the eyes, or a tense, very painful limb with numbness or tingling need emergency care, because the problem may be under the bruise, not in it. Photograph your bruises early, because they will not be there for long.
