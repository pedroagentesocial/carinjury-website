When people talk about back pain after a crash, they usually mean the lower back. But many patients point somewhere else: between the shoulder blades, or across the middle of the back where the bra strap or the shoulder belt sits. It aches when you sit, it pulls when you take a deep breath, and it gets worse at the end of the day.

This part of the spine, the **thoracic spine**, is built for stability more than movement, so it is usually the muscles around it that get strained. **Most upper and mid-back pain after a crash improves with time and activity, but a few causes need to be ruled out first, including fractures and pain that is not coming from the back at all.**

## Why the middle of the back hurts after a crash

Cleveland Clinic describes the upper back as the area from the base of your neck to the bottom of your rib cage. It moves less than the neck or the lower back, because it is attached to the ribs and is mainly there for structure and support. That is why, in a crash, the force is often taken by the muscles and soft tissue that hold the shoulder blades and the spine in place.

Common causes Cleveland Clinic lists for upper back pain include sprains and strains of soft tissue, herniated discs, fractured vertebrae and pinched nerves, plus conditions that make the spine more fragile, such as osteoporosis. The pain can feel sharp or dull, aching or burning, throbbing or like pins and needles, and it may come with muscle spasms.

The upper back also gets pain from the neck. Whiplash commonly causes tightness in the shoulders and upper back; read [what is whiplash](/en/blog/what-is-whiplash). And if the pain runs down an arm with tingling, see [numbness or tingling in your arms or hands](/en/blog/numbness-tingling-arms-hands-after-car-accident).

## When it is a fracture

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) explains on OrthoInfo that most spinal fractures happen in the thoracic and lumbar spine, or at the junction between them, and it lists car and motorcycle collisions among the high-energy causes. The main symptom is moderate to severe back pain that gets worse with movement. If the spinal cord or nerves are affected, there can be numbness, tingling or weakness in the arms or legs, or problems with the bladder or bowel.

OrthoInfo describes several patterns:

- **Compression fractures:** the front of the vertebra breaks and the back does not. They are usually stable, rarely cause nerve problems and are common in people with osteoporosis, which is why older adults can break a vertebra in a crash that seemed moderate. See [older adults after a car accident](/en/blog/older-adults-after-car-accident).
- **Burst fractures:** both the front and the back of the vertebra break; they can press on nerves and may be stable or unstable.
- **Flexion-distraction (Chance) fractures:** the vertebra is pulled apart. OrthoInfo notes they can happen in a head-on collision when the upper body is thrown forward while a lap belt holds the pelvis. They are typically unstable and often need surgery. The same mechanism can injure the organs in the abdomen; read [belly pain after a car accident](/en/blog/abdominal-pain-after-car-accident).

Diagnosis uses X-rays, CT and MRI depending on the injuries; see [X-ray, CT or MRI after a car accident](/en/blog/xray-mri-ct-after-car-accident). OrthoInfo says most stable compression and burst fractures can be treated without surgery, sometimes with a brace, followed by gradually increasing activity and rehabilitation. Back pain for several months is common afterward, and recovery is slow, but many people recover well.

## When the pain is not coming from the back

Cleveland Clinic warns that upper back pain can be referred pain from organs such as the heart, lungs, esophagus, kidneys or spleen. It lists the symptoms of a heart attack or a blood clot in the lungs, such as chest pain or trouble breathing, as reasons to call 911. After a crash, also think of the ribs and the chest wall: see [chest and rib pain after a car accident](/en/blog/chest-rib-pain-seatbelt-after-car-accident).

## What helps it heal

For the common, non-emergency causes, Cleveland Clinic lists rest, cold or heat packs, over-the-counter pain relievers such as acetaminophen, ibuprofen or naproxen, prescription anti-inflammatories or muscle relaxers, massage, and physical therapy to improve strength and posture. Read [ibuprofen or Tylenol after a car accident](/en/blog/pain-relievers-after-car-accident) before you choose one.

Short rest is fine, but the upper back responds best to movement: gentle mobility for the shoulder blades and the thoracic spine, breathing deeply, and then strengthening the muscles that hold your posture. Long hours hunched over a phone or a desk tend to keep it irritated. For how active care compares with passive treatments, see [heat, ultrasound or exercise: what the evidence says](/en/blog/passive-vs-active-care-after-car-accident).

## The short version

Pain between the shoulder blades or in the middle of the back after a crash is usually strained muscle and soft tissue, and it improves with time and the right activity. But the thoracic spine is where many spinal fractures happen, especially with osteoporosis or a lap-belt injury, and upper back pain can also come from the heart, lungs or abdomen. Get emergency care for the red-flag signs, get checked if it is not improving after a week, and keep a record of your symptoms; see [how to document your injuries for an insurance claim](/en/blog/how-to-document-injuries-insurance-claim). For lower back pain, read [back pain after a car accident](/en/blog/back-pain-after-car-accident).
