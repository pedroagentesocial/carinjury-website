It has been weeks since the crash. Your neck still hurts, you are behind at work, you cannot play with your kids the way you used to, and the calls with the insurance company never end. Lately you do not feel like seeing anyone, you are tired all the time and things you used to enjoy feel flat.

Frustration and bad days after a car accident are normal. **But when low mood, loss of interest or hopelessness last most of the day, nearly every day, for two weeks or more, it may be depression. Depression is a real medical condition, it is common after an injury that changes your daily life, and it is treatable.**

## Normal frustration or depression?

After a crash, almost everyone has some emotional reaction: irritability, worry about money, sadness about what you cannot do right now. That usually comes and goes and improves as your body heals. The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) describes depression as symptoms that are present **most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks**, and that affect how you feel, think and handle daily activities.

Other symptoms NIMH lists include trouble concentrating or making decisions, sleeping too much or too little, changes in appetite or weight, aches or pains without a clear physical cause, and thoughts of death or suicide. Not everyone has all of them.

## Why a crash can lead to depression

- **Pain that lasts.** NIMH notes that people with chronic medical conditions, including chronic pain, have a higher risk of depression, and that people who have depression together with another medical illness tend to have more severe symptoms of both. Pain wears down your mood, and low mood makes pain harder to cope with.
- **Sleep.** Pain and stress disrupt sleep, and poor sleep affects mood. Read [trouble sleeping after a car accident](/en/blog/trouble-sleeping-after-car-accident).
- **A head injury.** The CDC lists mood changes such as irritability, sadness and feeling more emotional among the symptoms of a concussion. If your mood changed after a blow to the head, tell your provider. See [concussion after a car accident](/en/blog/concussion-after-car-accident).
- **Losing your routine.** Time off work, money stress, not being able to exercise or care for your family the same way.
- **Fear and stress from the crash itself.** If what you feel is mostly fear, flashbacks or avoiding driving, read [fear of driving after a car accident](/en/blog/fear-of-driving-after-car-accident); anxiety and depression often show up together.

## What helps

NIMH explains that depression, even when it is severe, can be treated, and that the main treatments are **psychotherapy, medication or a combination of both**:

- **Talk therapy.** Approaches such as cognitive behavioral therapy help you change thought patterns and habits that keep depression going.
- **Medication.** Antidepressants prescribed by a doctor usually take **4 to 8 weeks** to work fully. Do not stop them on your own; talk to your doctor first. Tell your providers about every medicine you take, including pain medicines and muscle relaxers.
- **Treating the pain too.** Taking care of the physical injury, with a plan that gets you moving again, is part of taking care of your mood. See [passive vs active care](/en/blog/passive-vs-active-care-after-car-accident).
- **Small daily steps.** Gentle activity, time outside, a regular sleep schedule and staying in contact with people you trust. They do not replace treatment, but they help.

## Talk about it with your providers

Many patients only mention their back or neck at appointments. Tell your provider how your mood, sleep and energy have changed since the crash. It helps them see the whole picture, refer you to a mental health professional when needed and keep an accurate record: emotional effects of an injury are part of your medical history. See [why your medical records matter after a car accident](/en/blog/medical-records-after-car-accident).

## The short version

Feeling down after a car accident is common, especially when pain, poor sleep and money stress pile up. When low mood, loss of interest or hopelessness last most of the day, nearly every day, for two weeks or more, it may be depression, and it deserves treatment just like your neck or back. Therapy, medication or both work for many people, and treating the pain helps the mood. If you ever think about harming yourself, call or text 988 right away.
