The crash is over, the police report is done and you are standing next to your car trying to decide what comes next. Do you go to the emergency room? To the urgent care down the street? Home, to see how you feel tomorrow?

There is a simple way to think about it. **Symptoms that could threaten your life, your brain or your spine belong in the ER, and often in an ambulance. Everything else still deserves an evaluation in the first days, at urgent care, your doctor or a clinic that treats car accident injuries.** The one option that tends to go badly is doing nothing.

## Go to the ER (or call 911) when it could be serious

MedlinePlus, the National Library of Medicine's health site, says to call 911 for choking, stopped breathing, severe chest pain or pressure, or a seizure lasting over a minute, and to go to the emergency department or call 911 for trouble breathing, heavy bleeding, pain in the arm or jaw and similar warning signs. Its advice is plain: **do not delay.**

Two crash injuries deserve their own warning:

- **Head injuries.** The CDC lists danger signs after a blow or jolt to the head that mean 911 or the emergency department: seizures, not recognizing people or places, repeated nausea or vomiting, unusual behavior or growing confusion, loss of consciousness with increasing drowsiness, slurred speech, weakness or numbness, a headache that gets worse and does not go away, and one pupil larger than the other or double vision. You do not have to hit your head to have a concussion; read [concussion after a car accident](/en/blog/concussion-after-car-accident).
- **Spine injuries.** MedlinePlus lists the signs of a possible spinal cord injury: numbness or tingling that spreads down an arm or leg, weakness or difficulty walking, loss of bladder or bowel control, a stiff neck or neck pain, and a head held in an unusual position. Call 911, and do not bend, twist or lift the person's head or body, or let them get up and walk.

Belly pain after a crash, especially with a seatbelt mark, also belongs in the ER: see [belly pain after a car accident](/en/blog/abdominal-pain-after-car-accident).

If you are not sure, treat it as an emergency. The ER is built to sort that out, and under the federal EMTALA law, hospitals with emergency departments must provide a medical screening exam when someone asks for emergency care, regardless of the person's ability to pay, and stabilize an emergency condition or arrange an appropriate transfer.

## Urgent care: for the minor injuries

MedlinePlus lists minor injuries such as sprains, back pain, minor cuts and burns, minor broken bones and minor eye injuries among the problems urgent care can handle. It is a reasonable stop the same day or the next day when you have no red-flag signs but something clearly hurts, especially after hours.

Keep two things in mind. Urgent care is designed for a single visit, so ask for a copy of your visit notes and plan a follow-up with someone who will track your recovery. And tell them it was a car accident, so the visit is documented and billed as one.

## A clinic that treats car accident injuries: for the days and weeks after

Many crash injuries do not show up in the first hours. Neck and back pain, headaches and stiffness often appear a day or two later, when the adrenaline is gone; read [delayed symptoms after a car accident](/en/blog/delayed-symptoms-after-car-accident). A clinic that sees car accident patients every day is set up for this part: a full exam, imaging when it is needed (see [X-ray, CT or MRI after a car accident](/en/blog/xray-mri-ct-after-car-accident)), a treatment plan and notes written so your recovery is documented from the first visit.

## Why "I'll wait and see" is the riskiest choice

Waiting has a medical cost and an insurance cost. Medically, some serious injuries start quietly. On the claim side, a gap between the crash and your first visit gives the insurer room to argue the injury came from something else. Read [the 30-day gap: how missed appointments hurt injury claims](/en/blog/gaps-in-treatment-hurt-your-claim), and keep every record: see [how to document your injuries for an insurance claim](/en/blog/how-to-document-injuries-insurance-claim).

## Who pays for each of them in Utah

Utah's Personal Injury Protection (PIP) does not depend on which door you walk through. Utah Code § 31A-22-307 says PIP covers, up to at least $3,000 per person, the reasonable value of necessary medical, surgical, X-ray, dental, rehabilitation, **ambulance, hospital** and nursing services. Give the ER, the urgent care and the clinic your PIP claim number. For what happens when PIP runs out, see [who pays my medical bills after a car accident in Utah](/en/blog/who-pays-medical-bills-car-accident-utah).

## The short version

Possible head, spine, chest or belly injuries, heavy bleeding or trouble breathing go to the ER, and often by ambulance. Minor injuries can go to urgent care. Everything else, including "I feel fine," deserves an evaluation in the first days, ideally with someone who will follow your recovery. For the full first-day plan, read [what to do in the first 24 hours after a car accident](/en/blog/what-to-do-first-24-hours-after-accident).
