In the crash your body kept moving forward and the seatbelt stopped it. It saved you — but it did that by clamping the full force of the impact onto a strap that runs right across your collarbone and shoulder.

That is why shoulder pain is one of the most common complaints after an accident, and one of the most often misread: sometimes the problem is in the shoulder, and sometimes it is in the neck.

## Three ways a crash hurts the shoulder

1. **The seatbelt.** In a frontal impact, the diagonal strap concentrates force on the collarbone and the top of the shoulder. A bruise in the exact shape of the belt is common.
2. **The steering wheel or dashboard.** You instinctively grip the wheel hard or stiff-arm the dash. That force travels up the arm into the shoulder — the same mechanism as falling on an outstretched arm, a classic cause of rotator cuff tears.
3. **The door.** In a side impact, the shoulder on the struck side hits the door or the window frame.

## Is it the shoulder or the neck?

The nerve roots in your neck — especially at the C5 and C6 levels — supply sensation to the shoulder area. Whiplash or a herniated disc in the neck can feel like shoulder pain even when the shoulder itself is healthy.

Clues that the source is the neck: the pain changes when you move your head, there is tingling or numbness running down the arm, or the shoulder moves well but still hurts. That is why a good evaluation always checks both. Treating only the shoulder when the problem is in the neck is one of the most common reasons the pain does not get better.

## Which imaging you need

Not every sore shoulder needs an MRI on day one. An **X-ray** rules out fractures and AC joint separations. An **MRI** shows soft tissue — rotator cuff, labrum — and is ordered when the exam suggests a tear, or when pain and weakness do not improve with initial treatment. We cover this in depth in [X-ray, CT or MRI after a car accident](/en/blog/xray-mri-ct-after-car-accident).

## The good news about recovery

Most crash-related shoulder injuries are treated without surgery. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, nonsurgical treatment relieves pain and improves function in about 80 to 85% of patients with a rotator cuff tear. The key is starting early and not immobilizing the shoulder too long: a shoulder kept still for weeks tends to stiffen.

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>What to do if your shoulder hurts after the crash</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Photograph the seatbelt bruise the same day and the days after.</li>
    <li>Write down which movements hurt: combing your hair, reaching overhead, putting on a jacket, sleeping on that side.</li>
    <li>Tell the examiner if your neck also hurts or your arm tingles.</li>
    <li>Use ice for the first 48 hours for swelling, without keeping the shoulder completely still.</li>
    <li>Avoid carrying weight with that arm until you have a diagnosis.</li>
    <li>If you still cannot lift your arm well after 2–3 weeks, ask to be re-evaluated.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## The short version

The seatbelt protects you by concentrating crash forces on your shoulder, so pain there is common. What matters is knowing whether the problem is the shoulder, the neck or both — and acting on the red flags. With early treatment, the vast majority recover without surgery.
