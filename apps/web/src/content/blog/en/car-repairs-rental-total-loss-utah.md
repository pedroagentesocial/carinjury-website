After a crash, there are two claims running at the same time. One is about your body: PIP, your treatment, your recovery. The other is about your car: the estimate, the body shop, how you get to work meanwhile and, sometimes, the call telling you the car is "totaled."

The car claim moves faster and feels more urgent, and it is easy to accept the first number just to have it over with. You do not have to. **Utah's Insurance Department has a rule, R590-190, that sets minimum standards for how insurers handle these claims: how a total loss is valued, what an estimate must do, when a rental is paid and how your deductible is recovered.**

## The repair estimate and the shop

Under R590-190-11, an estimate prepared or approved by the insurer must reasonably be expected to repair the damage, and the insurer must give you a copy. It may suggest shops conveniently located for you, but it may not make you travel an unreasonable distance to get an estimate or to use a specific shop. If the insurer designates a shop, it must have the vehicle restored to its pre-loss condition, at no extra cost to you beyond the policy terms, within a reasonable time.

R590-190-12 adds that paying less than the cost of repairs is an unfair practice unless you agree or the policy allows it, and that an insurer may not require you to use only its own claim service.

### Aftermarket parts

Utah Code § 31A-22-319 says an insurer may not specify non-OEM aftermarket crash parts for an insured's car unless it gives written notice. When an estimate uses them, each one must be clearly identified, and your copy must carry a disclosure that they are not made by the vehicle's original manufacturer, must be at least equivalent in fit, quality and performance, and are warranted by their own manufacturer or distributor, not by your car's maker. For policies issued on or after October 1, 2026, insurers must also include a notice about aftermarket parts when the policy is issued and renewed. Read the estimate line by line before you sign off on it.

## A rental car, or "loss of use"

R590-190-12 requires the insurer to disclose all coverages that may apply, including loss of use. When there is coverage for it, R590-190-11 says the insurer must pay the reasonable cost of transportation or a substitute rental while the car is out of service for repair, or, on a total loss, until it makes a reasonable settlement offer. Those payments may not be held back while the insurer examines the claim, and they are paid in addition to the value of the vehicle.

Also ask about storage: if the car is at a tow yard, the insurer must give reasonable written notice before it stops paying storage, and a reasonable time to move the car.

## When the car is a total loss

R590-190-11 gives an insurer two ways to settle a total loss with its own insured, less any deductible:

- **A comparable replacement vehicle**, with taxes, license and transfer fees paid.
- **A cash settlement** based on the actual cost of a comparable vehicle, **including taxes and fees**, using at least two comparable vehicles, at least two dealer quotes, or a qualifying market-value source.

If the claim is against the other driver's insurer, R590-190-11 says the settlement is based on the market value or actual cost of a comparable vehicle at the time of the loss, including taxes and fees. Keep in mind the at-fault driver's property damage limit: § 31A-22-304 requires at least **$25,000** for policies issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2025. A newer car can be worth more than that; see [hit by an uninsured driver in Utah](/en/blog/hit-by-uninsured-driver-utah) for when the other side has little or no coverage.

## Your deductible

If your own insurer pays and then goes after the other driver's insurer (subrogation), R590-190-11 says it must include your deductible in that demand, and if it recovers money, your deductible comes back to you according to the rule's terms. If you share some of the fault, read [what if the accident was partly my fault](/en/blog/partly-at-fault-car-accident-utah).

## Timing, releases and deadlines

- **Your own insurer:** within 30 days after receiving a complete proof of loss, it must accept or deny, or tell you why it needs more time and, if you have no attorney or public adjuster, keep you updated in writing every 45 days (R590-190-10). A denial must be in writing and cite the policy provision it relies on.
- **Releases:** an insurer may not use a release printed on the back of a check to cut off other claims, and must give you a separate release document if you ask (R590-190-12). Be especially careful that a property check does not settle your injury claim.
- **Deadline to sue:** four years for property damage to your vehicle or property from a motor vehicle accident (Utah Code § 78B-2-307(3), referenced in § 78B-2-305). For the injury side, see [Utah car accident deadlines](/en/blog/utah-car-accident-claim-deadlines).

## The short version

Your car claim has its own rules in Utah. The insurer's estimate must actually fix the car, aftermarket parts must be identified, a rental or loss of use must be paid when there is coverage for it, and a total loss must be valued like a comparable vehicle, including taxes and fees. Ask for everything in writing, and keep the car claim separate from your injury claim. If you were a passenger, your injury claim follows different rules: read [injured as a passenger in a Utah car accident](/en/blog/passenger-injured-car-accident-utah).
