You were in the passenger seat. Maybe it was a friend driving you home, a relative taking you to work or a coworker on the way to lunch. You did not choose the speed, the lane or the moment to turn, and still you are the one with the neck pain and the ER bill.

The good news is that, as a passenger, you are usually the easiest person in the crash to cover. **Utah law puts the first layer of payment on the insurance of the car you were riding in, whether or not you own a car or have a policy of your own.** The questions are about the order, the limits and how to claim against the driver who caused it.

## Step 1: the PIP of the car you were in

Personal Injury Protection (PIP) pays your first medical bills and part of your lost wages, regardless of fault. Utah Code § 31A-22-308 lists who can receive PIP benefits. Besides the named insured and relatives who live in the same household, it includes **any other person injured in an accident while occupying the vehicle described in the policy, with the express or implied consent of the named insured.** In plain words: if you were riding in your friend's car with permission, your friend's PIP covers you.

And § 31A-22-309(4) settles the order when more than one policy could apply: **primary coverage is given by the policy insuring the vehicle in use during the accident.**

Under § 31A-22-307, PIP must include at least:

- **Medical expenses:** up to at least $3,000 per person for necessary medical, surgical, X-ray, dental, rehabilitation, ambulance, hospital and nursing services.
- **Lost income:** the lesser of $250 a week or 85% of lost gross income, for up to 52 consecutive weeks (the first three days are not paid unless the disability lasts more than two weeks).
- **Household services:** up to $20 a day for up to 365 days, for help with things you would have done yourself.

For more on how PIP works, see [who pays my medical bills after a car accident in Utah](/en/blog/who-pays-medical-bills-car-accident-utah).

## Step 2: your own policy, if you have one

The same § 31A-22-308 also covers the named insured, and relatives who live in the insured's household, when injured in an accident involving **any** motor vehicle. So if you, or a relative you live with, have your own Utah policy, it can also come into play, but behind the policy of the car you were riding in.

One exclusion to know: under § 31A-22-309(2)(a)(i), an insurer may exclude PIP for an injury the insured suffers while occupying **another vehicle owned by the insured or a resident family member that is not insured under the policy.** If the car you were riding in belongs to your household and had no insurance, ask an attorney before assuming your own policy will pay.

## Step 3: the driver who caused the crash

PIP is only the first layer. Pain and suffering, and the bills and wages PIP does not cover, are claimed against the driver who caused the crash, through that driver's liability insurance. As a passenger, that can be the **other** driver, **your own** driver, or both, if each made a mistake.

Claiming against the driver of your own car can feel uncomfortable when it is a friend or a relative. Keep in mind that the claim is made against that driver's liability coverage, which exists precisely for this. An attorney can handle the communication so it does not become a personal conversation.

Two Utah rules shape this claim:

- **The threshold.** Under § 31A-22-309(1), a person covered by PIP can only claim general damages (such as pain and suffering) if the injuries include death, dismemberment, permanent disability or impairment based on objective findings, permanent disfigurement, **a bone fracture**, or **medical expenses over $3,000.**
- **The limits.** Under § 31A-22-304, policies issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2025 must carry at least **$30,000 for one person and $65,000 for two or more people** per accident in bodily injury liability. If the car was full of injured passengers and the at-fault driver only has the minimum, everyone may be sharing that same $65,000. That is one reason to bring your own insurance information to your attorney: see [hit by an uninsured driver in Utah](/en/blog/hit-by-uninsured-driver-utah) for how uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage works.

## Uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage as a passenger

Under § 31A-22-305, the "covered persons" of uninsured motorist (UM) coverage include **any person occupying a motor vehicle referred to in the policy.** So if the at-fault driver had no insurance, the UM coverage of the car you were riding in can apply to you. The statute also has rules about when your own UM can add a layer, and which coverage is primary and which is secondary. Those rules are detailed, so this is a good question for an attorney.

## Fault, and why passengers are rarely blamed

A passenger does not control the car, so the fault question is usually between the drivers. If an insurer tries to argue you share blame, read [what if the accident was partly my fault? ## The short version

As a passenger in Utah, the PIP of the car you were riding in pays first, even if you have no insurance of your own; your own policy, if you have one, comes after it. Beyond PIP, you can claim against whichever driver caused the crash, including your own driver, once you meet the § 31A-22-309 threshold, keeping in mind that several passengers may share one policy limit. Get care early, keep every bill and note, and see [how to document your injuries for an insurance claim](/en/blog/how-to-document-injuries-insurance-claim).
