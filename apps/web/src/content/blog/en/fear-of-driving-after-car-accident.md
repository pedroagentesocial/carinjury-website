Your hands sweat when you start the car. You brake hard when someone comes up behind you. You would rather take a ten-minute detour than drive through the intersection where you were hit.

None of that means you are overreacting. After a crash, the brain learns that driving is dangerous, and it takes time to unlearn it. The question is not whether fear is normal — it is — but whether it is fading over the weeks or settling in.

## What is normal in the first weeks

After a frightening event, almost everyone has some stress reaction: sleeping badly, replaying the moment of impact, feeling on guard in the car, getting irritated more easily, not wanting to talk about it. According to the U.S. National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH), symptoms like these are common right after a traumatic event, and for most people **they fade on their own over time**.

## When it stops being normal

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is considered when symptoms last **more than a month** and are severe enough to interfere with daily life: work, relationships, your ability to get around town. The symptoms fall into these groups:

## Pain and anxiety feed each other

This rarely gets said: the physical pain from the crash and the anxiety are not separate problems. Pain reminds you of the accident every day; anxious tension stiffens your neck and back; poor sleep makes both worse. That is why patients who only treat one half tend to take longer to recover from the other.

If you notice in rehab that fear is holding you back — you avoid driving to your appointments, you brace your whole body during exercises — tell whoever is treating you. It is part of your recovery, not a separate issue.

## Getting back behind the wheel, step by step

Avoidance brings relief today but makes the fear bigger tomorrow. What works is gradual exposure: approaching driving in steps you can tolerate, repeating each one until the anxiety drops before moving to the next.

<div class="pa-timeline">
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Step 1</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">As a passenger</p>
    <p>Short rides with someone you trust at the wheel, at quiet times and on familiar streets.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Step 2</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">You drive, no traffic</p>
    <p>An empty parking lot or your own block early on a Sunday. Just starting, braking, turning.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Step 3</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Residential streets</p>
    <p>Short, familiar trips in daylight, ideally with company at first.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Step 4</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Busy roads</p>
    <p>Everyday routes like your commute or the grocery store, first at low-traffic hours and then at normal ones.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Step 5</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">The freeway and the crash site</p>
    <p>I-15 or I-215, at night or in the rain if that scares you, and finally driving past where you crashed.</p>
  </div>
</div>

If a step still brings panic after several tries, it is not a lack of willpower: it is the sign that this is worth doing with professional help.

## Which treatments work

According to NIMH, the main treatments for PTSD are **psychotherapy** — especially trauma-focused approaches such as cognitive behavioral therapy and exposure therapy — and, in some cases, **medication** prescribed by a doctor. Many people improve within a limited number of sessions.

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>What to do if the fear is not fading</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Track your emotional symptoms the same way as physical ones: nightmares, routes you avoid, hours of sleep.</li>
    <li>Tell your doctor or the clinic treating you: they can refer you to a mental health professional.</li>
    <li>Do not self-medicate with alcohol or sleeping pills without a prescription.</li>
    <li>Keep your physical treatment appointments: sleeping and moving better also lowers anxiety.</li>
    <li>If you have an attorney, let them know: the emotional effects of a crash are part of your injuries too.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## The short version

Being afraid to drive after an accident is normal, and for most people it fades within a few weeks. If it is still disrupting your life after a month, it may be PTSD — and it is treatable. Get back behind the wheel step by step, care for body and mind together, and ask for help without shame.
