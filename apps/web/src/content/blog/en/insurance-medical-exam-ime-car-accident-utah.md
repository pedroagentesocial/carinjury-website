Weeks or months into your recovery, a letter arrives: the insurance company, or the lawyer for the driver who hit you, wants you to be examined by a doctor they chose. It is often called an **"independent medical exam," or IME**, and many patients walk in nervous, unsure whether they have to go and what the doctor is looking for.

Knowing the rules takes most of the stress out of it. **This doctor is not your treating provider, and in a Utah lawsuit the exam happens only by court order, with conditions the court sets and with your right, as a general rule, to record it.**

## What this exam is, and what it is not

Your treating providers examine you to diagnose and treat you. The doctor in this exam is chosen and paid by the party that asked for it, to give an opinion about your injuries: what caused them, whether your treatment was necessary and whether you have recovered. It is an evaluation, not care.

Even the word "independent" is debated. The committee that drafted Utah's Rule 35 notes that a medical exam is not a matter of right, and it recommends that the parties and the court avoid the phrase "independent medical examiner," using neutral names like "medical examiner" or "Rule 35 examiner" instead.

## Once there is a lawsuit: Utah Rule 35

Rule 35 of the Utah Rules of Civil Procedure governs physical and mental exams in a civil case:

<div class="pa-timeline">
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">The motion</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">The other side has to ask the court</p>
    <p>The exam can be ordered when your physical or mental condition is "in controversy," but only on a motion for good cause. The examiner must be suitably licensed or certified.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">The order</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">The court sets the rules of the exam</p>
    <p>The order must specify the time, place, manner, conditions and scope of the exam, and who will perform it. Read it with your attorney before the appointment.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">The exam</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">You may record it</p>
    <p>You can record the exam by audio or video unless the party requesting it shows the recording would unduly interfere. The committee notes say a professional is not required, and that a person running the equipment must do so without interfering.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Within 60 days</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">The report has to be shared</p>
    <p>The requesting party must disclose the examiner's detailed written report, with findings, test results and diagnoses, within 60 days of the exam or 7 days before fact discovery closes, whichever comes first.</p>
  </div>
</div>

If a party does not obey a Rule 35 order, the court can impose the sanctions allowed under Rule 37(b), although the failure cannot be treated as contempt of court. In practice: once there is an order, go, and go on time.

## Before a lawsuit: your policy and your claim

Rule 35 applies to court cases. Before a lawsuit, a request usually comes from an insurer handling a claim, often your own PIP carrier. Whether you must attend, and what happens if you do not, then depends on the language of your policy and the facts of your claim. That is why it is worth asking for the request, and the policy provision it relies on, in writing, and having your attorney review it.

## If PIP stops paying after the exam

Sometimes the examiner's report says you have recovered, or that further treatment is not necessary, and PIP payments stop. Utah Code § 31A-22-309(5) sets the payment rules for PIP:

- Benefits are paid monthly as expenses are incurred, and are **overdue if not paid within 30 days** after the insurer receives reasonable proof of the fact and amount of the expenses.
- Overdue expenses bear **interest of 1.5% per month.**
- You can bring an action in contract to recover them, and if the insurer is required to pay overdue benefits and interest, it must also pay **a reasonable attorney's fee.**

The best answer to an exam report you disagree with is usually your own records: consistent visits, clear notes and your treating provider's opinion. See [your medical records after a car accident](/en/blog/medical-records-after-car-accident), and if PIP runs out, [treatment with no money upfront](/en/blog/treatment-with-no-money-upfront).

## How to prepare

- **Know your own history.** Review when the crash happened, your symptoms since then, your treatment and any prior injuries. If you had back or neck problems before, read [how a pre-existing condition affects your claim](/en/blog/pre-existing-condition-car-accident-utah).
- **Be honest and specific.** Do not exaggerate, and do not minimize to look tough. Describe what you can and cannot do, with examples from your daily life.
- **Answer what is asked.** The exam is not the place to argue who caused the crash. For the same reason you are careful with adjusters, read [what to say to the insurance adjuster](/en/blog/what-to-say-insurance-adjuster).
- **Bring a list of your medications** and the names of your providers.
- **Write notes right after:** what time it started and ended, what tests were done, how long the doctor spent with you and anything that caused pain.
- **Keep going to your own treatment.** Do not stop because an exam is coming; a gap hurts more than it helps. See [the 30-day gap](/en/blog/gaps-in-treatment-hurt-your-claim).

## The short version

The exam with the insurer's or the defense's doctor is an evaluation, not treatment. Once there is a lawsuit in Utah, Rule 35 requires a court order for good cause that spells out the conditions, lets you record the exam unless that would unduly interfere, and requires the detailed report to be disclosed within 60 days. Before a lawsuit, what applies depends on your policy, so get the request in writing and talk to your attorney. And if PIP stops paying, Utah law sets a 30-day payment rule, interest and attorney's fees for overdue benefits. Keep documenting your recovery: see [how to document your injuries for an insurance claim](/en/blog/how-to-document-injuries-insurance-claim).
