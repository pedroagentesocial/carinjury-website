"There's barely a scratch on the bumper."

It is the first thing the adjuster says, and by then you have often started to doubt yourself. If the car is fine, how injured can you really be?

The honest answer from the biomechanics literature is: **the two are not measuring the same thing.** Your bumper is engineered to survive a low-speed hit. Your cervical spine is not.

Delta-V is the *change* in your vehicle's speed during impact — not how fast anyone was driving. Peer-reviewed biomechanical work documents clinically important cervical injury at delta-V values as low as 5 to 8 mph, with case reports lower still. Meanwhile, a modern bumper system can absorb a delta-V of 8 to 10 mph with no visible damage to the outer cover and only minor deformation in the energy absorber behind it.

That gap — injury below, visible damage above — is the entire argument, and it is why "no damage" is not the medical evidence it is presented as.

## What happens in the half second you did not see

<div class="pa-timeline">
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">0–50 ms</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">The seat pushes your torso forward</p>
    <p>The vehicle and seat accelerate. Your head, which is not attached to the seat, does not move yet. The neck begins to load.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">50–150 ms</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">S-curve and hyperextension</p>
    <p>The cervical spine deforms into an S shape — lower segments extend while upper segments still flex — then the head snaps backward beyond its normal range.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">150–300 ms</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Rebound into flexion</p>
    <p>The head whips forward. Volunteer studies at impacts of 3.6–6.8 mph measured tangential head accelerations exceeding 10 G in this window.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">The whole time</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Your muscles never got involved</p>
    <p>Voluntary muscle response is too slow for a 200–500 ms event. The forces are absorbed by passive tissue: ligaments, joint capsules and discs — the structures that heal slowly and scar stiff.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="pa-note">
  <span class="pa-note-icon">🚗</span>
  <div>
    <p><strong>Counterintuitive but consistent:</strong> a stiff bumper that does not crumple transmits more energy to the occupant, not less. Crush damage is energy the <em>car</em> absorbed. When the car absorbs nothing, the occupant does.</p>
  </div>

## Who gets hurt most in a "minor" crash

Risk in low-speed collisions is not evenly distributed. Factors repeatedly associated with worse outcomes include:

<div class="pa-compare">
  <div class="pa-col pa-col-bad">
    <h4>Increases risk</h4>
    <ul>
      <li>Head turned or leaning at the moment of impact</li>
      <li>Being unaware the hit was coming (no bracing)</li>
      <li>Head restraint set too low or too far back</li>
      <li>Pre-existing neck arthritis or a prior whiplash injury</li>
      <li>Being the struck vehicle rather than the striking one</li>
      <li>A smaller vehicle struck by a heavier one</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-col pa-col-good">
    <h4>Reduces risk</h4>
    <ul>
      <li>Head restraint level with the top of the head, close to it</li>
      <li>Upright seat back, head against the restraint</li>
      <li>Seeing the impact coming and being braced</li>
      <li>Modern active head-restraint seats</li>
      <li>Early evaluation and early active treatment</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

## What to do when your car looks fine and you do not

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>Practical steps</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Photograph the bumper anyway — including the underside and the energy absorber if a shop opens it up.</li>
    <li>Keep the repair estimate. Internal damage often exceeds what the outer cover shows.</li>
    <li>Get evaluated within 72 hours; document range of motion and neurological findings, not just "neck pain".</li>
    <li>Describe the mechanism accurately: where your head was, whether you saw it coming, seat and head-restraint position.</li>
    <li>Do not let "no visible damage" become the reason you skip treatment — that gap gets used against you later.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## The short version

Vehicle damage measures what happened to a bumper. Symptoms, range of motion and neurological findings measure what happened to you. The research is clear that injury occurs well below the threshold of visible damage, so let a clinical exam decide whether you are hurt — not a photo of a bumper.

If your neck symptoms started after a low-speed hit, [what is whiplash](/en/blog/what-is-whiplash) explains the injury itself, and [delayed symptoms after a car accident](/en/blog/delayed-symptoms-after-car-accident) explains why they may not have appeared until day three.
