In a sudden stop, the lap belt does exactly what it is supposed to do: it holds your pelvis in the seat. But the body keeps moving forward over it, and the force goes into the abdomen. Hours later you notice the stripe of a bruise across your stomach, or a pain on the left side under the ribs that you blame on the belt.

Most belly soreness after a crash is bruised muscle. **But the abdomen is also where some of the most dangerous crash injuries hide, because the organs inside can bleed or tear without much pain at first.** That is why belly pain after an accident is a reason to be examined, not to wait.

## How a crash injures the abdomen

The Merck Manual explains that blunt abdominal injuries come from a direct blow or from a sudden decrease in speed, and it names motor vehicle crashes as an example. In a car, the blow can come from the lap belt, the steering wheel or the door in a side impact.

A review in *BMC Surgery* describes how the lap belt can act as a fulcrum: the body folds forward around it, and the force can injure the intestine, the lumbar spine and other abdominal organs. That combination is often called the "seatbelt complex." None of this means you should ride without a belt. The belt is what keeps you from being thrown into the dashboard or out of the car; the point is to take its mark seriously.

## The seatbelt mark across your stomach

A bruise across the lower belly in the shape of the lap belt is called the **seatbelt sign**. It is a clue about where the force went, not a measure of how bad the injury is. Merck says it plainly: the presence of a bruise does not necessarily reflect the severity of the abdominal injury.

The studies collected in the *BMC Surgery* review show why doctors pay attention to it:

## The spleen: pain on the left that can travel to the shoulder

The spleen sits on the upper left side of the abdomen, under the ribs. According to the Merck Manual, the pain of a spleen injury usually begins in the left side of the abdomen just below the rib cage, and sometimes in the left shoulder. The abdominal muscles may tighten and feel rigid. With significant blood loss, blood pressure falls, and people can feel light-headed, have blurred vision, become confused or faint.

The part people do not expect is the timing. Merck explains that a bruise (hematoma) of the spleen may not bleed at first, but it can rupture in the first few days after the injury, and sometimes not for weeks or months. A spleen that is already enlarged, for example from mononucleosis, is more prone to injury.

The good news: Merck notes that most small and moderate spleen injuries can heal without surgery, with treatment in the hospital and sometimes blood transfusions. When surgery is needed, the spleen is usually removed, and because that raises the risk of dangerous infections, precautions are needed afterward.

## The bowel: the injury that can take hours to show

Tears of the intestine or of the tissue that carries its blood vessels (the mesentery) are less common than spleen and liver injuries, but they are harder to catch. Merck describes the pain of a small intestine tear as minimal at first, then steadily worse. The *BMC Surgery* review adds that signs often take a number of hours to become clear, that the physical exam is "notoriously inaccurate" in blunt abdominal trauma, and that delays of even 6 to 8 hours can lead to a serious infection from the contents of the bowel.

That is why "I felt okay at the scene" does not settle anything. Read [delayed symptoms after a car accident](/en/blog/delayed-symptoms-after-car-accident) for the other injuries that show up late.

## How doctors check

- **Ultrasound** at the bedside is quick and useful to find severe bleeding, according to Merck.
- **CT scan** takes longer but gives more precise images, and it can also show fractures of the spine or pelvis. The *BMC Surgery* review calls CT the main tool for stable patients, while noting that a bowel injury can be subtle on the first scan.
- **Urine and blood tests:** a urinalysis looks for blood in the urine, and a blood count gives a baseline to compare against.

When a scan shows an injury to the spleen or liver, Merck explains that patients are usually admitted and examined every few hours to make sure the bleeding has stopped, sometimes with repeat imaging. For what each scan does, see [X-ray, CT or MRI after a car accident](/en/blog/xray-mri-ct-after-car-accident).

## After you go home

If you were checked and sent home, keep watching for the warning signs in the red box, especially in the first days. Avoid heavy lifting or hard exercise until your doctor clears you. Before taking anti-inflammatories, ask: some pain relievers are not a good idea when bleeding is a concern; read [ibuprofen or Tylenol after a car accident](/en/blog/pain-relievers-after-car-accident).

## The short version

The lap belt protects you, but in a hard stop it can transmit force into the abdomen. The spleen and liver are the organs most often injured, a spleen bruise can bleed days or even weeks later, and a torn bowel can take hours to show. A belt mark across the belly is a reason to be checked, and worsening pain, a hard abdomen, left shoulder pain or dizziness is a reason to go to the ER. Write down your symptoms as they appear; see [how to document your injuries for an insurance claim](/en/blog/how-to-document-injuries-insurance-claim).
