A few days after the crash, you rolled over in bed and the whole room started to spin. Or maybe it is something quieter: you feel off balance in the grocery store aisle, or lightheaded when you stand up.

Dizziness is a common complaint after a car accident, and it is not always the same problem. **The word "dizzy" covers several different sensations**, and each one points to a different cause. Telling your provider exactly what you feel is the first step to the right treatment.

## What kind of dizzy?

Before anything else, try to describe the feeling. Providers usually separate:

- **Vertigo:** a false sense of spinning or movement.
- **Imbalance:** feeling unsteady on your feet, as if you might fall, without spinning.
- **Lightheadedness:** feeling faint or "woozy," as if you are about to pass out.

According to MedlinePlus, from the U.S. National Library of Medicine, BPPV is the most common type of vertigo, and a head injury, even a slight bump, raises the risk. Neck-related dizziness is less well understood and is usually diagnosed after other causes have been ruled out.

## How it is evaluated

The provider asks what you feel, when it started, how long each episode lasts and what triggers it. Then they check your eye movements, balance, neck and nervous system. For positional vertigo, a common test is the **Dix-Hallpike maneuver**: the provider moves your head and body into specific positions and watches your eyes.

## Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause:

- **BPPV:** MedlinePlus describes the **Epley maneuver**, a series of head movements that moves the loose crystals back into place, as the most effective treatment. It may need to be repeated. Medicines for spinning often do not help much with this type of vertigo.
- **Concussion and imbalance:** vestibular rehabilitation, a type of physical therapy with eye, head and balance exercises, helps the brain readjust.
- **Neck-related dizziness:** treatment for the neck injury, with exercises for mobility, posture and control of head movements.
- **Medicines:** if a new medicine is making you dizzy, talk with the doctor who prescribed it. Do not stop it on your own.

## Stay safe while you recover

- **Do not drive** while you have episodes of dizziness or vertigo.
- **Get up slowly:** sit on the edge of the bed for a moment before standing.
- **Prevent falls at home:** clear rugs and clutter, use night lights and hold on to the rail on stairs.
- **Do not just avoid moving:** after the first days, staying very still can make the brain slower to adapt. Follow your provider's plan.

## The short version

Dizziness after a crash can come from the inner ear, a concussion, the neck or your medicines, and each one is treated differently. Dizziness with weakness, slurred speech, vision changes, a severe headache or fainting is an emergency. For everything else: describe exactly what you feel, get evaluated and do not drive until the episodes are under control.
