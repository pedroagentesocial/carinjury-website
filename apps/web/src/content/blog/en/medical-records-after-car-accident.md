After a crash, your care is spread across places: the ambulance, the ER, an imaging center, your primary doctor, a chiropractor or a physical therapist. Each one keeps its own chart and its own bills. Sooner or later the insurer, your attorney or a new specialist will want all of it in one place, and the person who usually has to put it together is you.

**Under federal law (HIPAA), you have the right to get copies of your medical and billing records, within a set deadline and at a limited cost.** Knowing the rules makes the process much faster.

## Why your records matter so much

Your records are where the story of your injury is told in writing: what you said happened, what hurt, what was found, what was done and what it cost. Adjusters read them as a timeline, and gaps or inconsistencies stand out (see [the 30-day gap](/en/blog/gaps-in-treatment-hurt-your-claim)). If you had back or neck problems before, your older records are what show the difference between before and after; read [how a pre-existing condition affects your claim](/en/blog/pre-existing-condition-car-accident-utah). And PIP and the other insurers pay against those records and bills; see [who pays my medical bills after a car accident in Utah](/en/blog/who-pays-medical-bills-car-accident-utah).

## What you can ask for

The HIPAA Privacy Rule gives you access to the "designated record set," which for a health care provider includes **the medical records and the billing records** it keeps about you. In practice, after a crash, ask each provider for:

- **Visit notes,** from the ER, urgent care, your primary doctor and each therapy visit.
- **Imaging reports,** and the images themselves if a specialist will need to review them.
- **An itemized bill,** with dates of service, each charge and what has been paid.
- **Referrals, prescriptions and work notes,** which support time off and lost wages.

## How to ask

<div class="pa-timeline">
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Step 1</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Ask for their form, or write it yourself</p>
    <p>A provider may require requests in writing, as long as it tells you so. Include your full name, date of birth, the dates of care, what you want and how you want it delivered.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Step 2</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Pick the format</p>
    <p>If the records are kept electronically and you ask for an electronic copy, the provider must give it in the electronic form and format you request if it is readily producible that way; otherwise, in a readable electronic format you both agree on.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Step 3</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Send it straight to your attorney if you want</p>
    <p>You can direct the provider to send the copy to another person. The request must be in writing, signed by you, and clearly name that person and where to send it.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Within 30 days</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">The provider must act</p>
    <p>The deadline is 30 days from receiving your request. It can be extended once, by up to 30 days, only if the provider gives you a written explanation of the delay and the date it will act, within the first 30 days.</p>
  </div>
</div>

Write down when and how you sent each request, and keep a copy. If the deadline passes, a polite follow-up that cites the date usually moves things along.

## What they can charge

HIPAA allows a **reasonable, cost-based fee**, and it can only cover:

  <table>

      <tr><th>Can be included</th><th>What it means</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Labor for copying</td><td>The work of making the paper or electronic copy</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Supplies</td><td>Paper, or portable media such as a CD or USB drive, if you ask for the copy that way</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Postage</td><td>Only if you ask for it to be mailed</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Preparing a summary</td><td>Only if you agreed to receive a summary instead of, or along with, the full record</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

Ask about the fee before the copy is made, so there are no surprises.

## If something in your chart is wrong

Read your records. Look for the date and description of the crash, every body part that hurt, and what you said about your symptoms. If something is wrong or missing, you have the right to ask the provider to amend it. The provider may require the request in writing with a reason, and must act within 60 days, with one possible extension of up to 30 days. If it refuses, you can submit a written statement explaining why you disagree, and the provider must append it to, or link it with, your record.

Some things are easier to fix at the visit: if you mention new symptoms, ask that they be written down that day. See [delayed symptoms after a car accident](/en/blog/delayed-symptoms-after-car-accident).

## The short version

Your medical and billing records are yours to request. Providers must act within 30 days (with one extension of up to 30 days, in writing), can give you electronic copies, can send them directly to your attorney at your written request, and can only charge a reasonable, cost-based fee for copying, supplies, postage or an agreed summary. Request them from every provider, read them, and ask for corrections if something is wrong. Together with your photos and notes, they are the core of your claim: see [how to document your injuries for an insurance claim](/en/blog/how-to-document-injuries-insurance-claim).
