A few days after the crash, the adjuster asks whether you have the police report. You have a small card with a case number on it, and no idea where the report lives, who can see it or whether it says what really happened.

The crash report will not decide your claim on its own, but **it is one of the first documents every insurer reads, and errors in it are easier to fix early than late.** Here is how it works in Utah.

The Utah Highway Safety Office describes a reportable crash as one involving a motor vehicle, injury or death or at least $2,500 in apparent property damage, on a highway or a road or parking area open to the public. With today's repair costs, even a "small" bumper hit can pass $2,500. For the full checklist at the scene, see [what to do in the first 24 hours](/en/blog/what-to-do-first-24-hours-after-accident).

## What goes into the report

Under Utah Code 41-6a-402, the report must include enough detail to show the cause of the crash, the conditions at the time, the vehicles involved and the name, address and phone number of each person involved, including witnesses. A witness can ask that their address and phone be kept off the report; the officer then keeps them in a separate record that a party can obtain in a lawsuit about the crash.

The law also counts much more than the written form as part of the "accident report": witness statements, photographs, **body camera and dash camera video**, diagrams and the officer's field notes. Those extras can matter as much as the report itself.

## Who can get the full report

Utah law treats crash reports as protected records. The agency that investigated must release the unredacted report to:

- **Anyone involved in the crash** (witnesses excluded) and anyone who suffered loss or injury in it.
- **Their agent, parent or legal guardian.** "Agent" includes your attorney, your insurer, a hospital emergency room that treated you for the crash, or anyone you give signed permission.
- Law enforcement and government agencies, and, in limited form, the news media.

The agency must also tell you **whether each driver and vehicle had insurance, and the name of the insurer**. That matters if the other driver gave you wrong information or no information at all; see [hit by an uninsured driver in Utah](/en/blog/hit-by-uninsured-driver-utah).

## Read it as soon as you get it

Mistakes happen at busy crash scenes. Check:

- **Names, license plates, vehicles and insurance.** A wrong policy number can delay your PIP or the other driver's claim.
- **Date, time, location and direction of travel.**
- **The narrative and the diagram.** Do they match what happened?
- **Whether injuries were noted.** Many people say "I'm fine" at the scene and feel the pain the next day. That is common, and it is why your medical records matter so much.
- **Witnesses.** Were they listed?

If something is factually wrong, contact the agency that wrote the report and ask how to request a correction or a supplemental report, and bring proof (photos, your insurance card, your registration). An officer's opinion about fault is a different matter: you can disagree with it, but the place to argue that is with the insurer, with your attorney's help. Read [what if the accident was partly my fault?](/en/blog/partly-at-fault-car-accident-utah).

## Why strangers sometimes call you after a crash

Utah law makes it a class A misdemeanor to knowingly obtain a crash report you are not entitled to, or to use information from one to market services, including legal representation. If someone you did not contact calls about your crash, be careful what you share, and never give a recorded statement without thinking it through. See [what to say to the insurance adjuster](/en/blog/what-to-say-insurance-adjuster).

## The short version

In Utah, the police must be notified right away after a crash with injuries or about $2,500 or more in property damage. The investigating officer files the report with the state within 10 days of finishing the investigation. You, your attorney, your insurer and anyone you authorize can get the full report from the agency that investigated, and a written request gets you the report plus photos and video within 10 business days. Read it as soon as you have it, fix factual errors early, and remember that the report matters to the insurer but your medical records are what prove your injuries.
