When a car accident claim finally settles, one of the first worries is the tax bill. Will the IRS take a share? Do you have to report it? Should you set money aside?

For most people hurt in a crash, the news is good. **Under federal law, damages received on account of personal physical injuries or physical sickness are not included in your income. The exceptions are specific: punitive damages, interest, and the part that repays medical expenses you already deducted on an earlier return.** This article covers federal income tax; your state return and your own situation are questions for a tax professional.

## The rule: physical injury damages are not income

Section 104(a)(2) of the Internal Revenue Code excludes from gross income "the amount of any damages (other than punitive damages)" received on account of personal physical injuries or physical sickness, whether by lawsuit or by agreement. IRS Publication 4345 puts it in plain words: if you receive a settlement for personal physical injuries or physical sickness and did not take an itemized deduction for medical expenses related to it in prior years, **the full amount is non-taxable.**

A car accident injury claim, with whiplash, a herniated disc, a broken bone or a concussion, is a claim for physical injuries. That is why most injury settlements from a crash are not reported as income.

### Emotional distress

The tax code says emotional distress is not, by itself, a physical injury. But IRS Publication 525 explains that damages for emotional distress **due to a physical injury** are treated as received for the physical injury, so you do not include them in income. Anxiety, poor sleep or fear of driving after a crash that injured you fall on that side. Emotional distress damages that do not come from a physical injury are a different case, and are generally taxable except for the related medical care.

### Lost wages

This is where people get confused. Publication 525's general list of ordinary income includes compensation for lost wages "in most cases." But the IRS page on settlements and judgments states that compensatory damages, **including lost wages**, received on account of a personal physical injury are generally excludable from gross income. In other words, lost wages inside a physical injury settlement are generally treated like the rest of the injury damages. If your wage loss is large, ask a tax professional to review how it is described. PIP wage benefits during your recovery are a separate topic; see [going back to work after an accident](/en/blog/back-to-work-lost-wages-car-accident-utah).

### Medical expenses you already deducted

Section 104(a) has an exception for amounts attributable to medical deductions taken under section 213 in a prior year. Publication 4345 explains that you include in income the part of the settlement that covers medical expenses you deducted in an earlier year, **to the extent the deduction gave you a tax benefit**, and points to the "Recoveries" section of Publication 525 to figure the amount. If you never itemized those medical bills, this exception does not apply to you.

## Punitive damages and interest

Punitive damages punish the person who caused the crash rather than compensate you. Section 104(a)(2) excludes them from the exclusion, and Publication 525 says they are ordinary income in most cases, even if they relate to a physical injury. The tax code has a narrow exception for certain wrongful death cases. Interest on an award is also ordinary income. In a typical car accident settlement there are no punitive damages, but a judgment after trial can include interest.

## Attorney fees

If your settlement is not taxable, the attorney's share usually does not create a tax issue for you either. Publication 525 lists attorney fees and costs as income only "where the underlying recovery is included in gross income." The IRS page on settlements also notes that the payer may have to report attorney fees on information returns. If you receive a Form 1099 you did not expect, show it to your attorney or a tax preparer.

## The short version

Under federal law, the money you receive for a physical injury from a car accident, including pain and suffering, medical bills, emotional distress that flows from the injury and, generally, lost wages, is not taxable income. Punitive damages and interest are, and so is the part that repays medical expenses you deducted before with a tax benefit. How the settlement is written matters, so review it with your attorney. And if your providers are waiting to be paid out of the settlement, read [how letters of protection and medical liens work](/en/blog/treatment-with-no-money-upfront).
