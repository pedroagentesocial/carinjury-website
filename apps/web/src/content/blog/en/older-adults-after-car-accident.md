Your mom was in a car accident. "It was nothing, just a scare," she says on the phone. But two days later she seems more tired, a little confused, and she does not want to walk to the kitchen because her side hurts.

In older adults, a crash that seems minor can have bigger consequences. **The same impact affects a 75-year-old body differently than a 30-year-old one**, and the warning signs are often quieter. This guide is for patients and for the family members who look after them.

## Why older adults are at higher risk

- **Bones.** With osteoporosis, a bone can break with much less force. Ribs, wrists, hips and the spine are especially vulnerable. The seatbelt mark across the chest deserves more attention: see [chest and rib pain after a car accident](/en/blog/chest-rib-pain-seatbelt-after-car-accident).
- **Medications.** Blood thinners (such as warfarin, apixaban or clopidogrel) increase the risk of bleeding inside the head after a blow, and that bleeding can grow slowly over hours or days.
- **Less obvious symptoms.** Pain, dizziness or confusion get blamed on age, on existing conditions or on "being nervous." According to the CDC, signs of a concussion in older adults can be missed or mistaken for other problems, such as dementia.
- **Slower recovery.** Healing takes longer, and a few days in bed can cause loss of strength, balance and independence.

Read more about [concussion after a car accident](/en/blog/concussion-after-car-accident).

## What family members should watch for in the following days

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>Checklist for the first two weeks</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Changes in memory, mood, speech or behavior.</li>
    <li>More drowsiness than usual or trouble waking up.</li>
    <li>New pain when breathing, walking, getting out of bed or sitting down.</li>
    <li>Unsteadiness or dizziness: the risk of falling at home goes up after a crash.</li>
    <li>Eating or drinking less than usual.</li>
    <li>Not taking their usual medicines, or taking pain medicine incorrectly.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

If you notice any of these changes, call their doctor or go to the ER. Write down what you see and when it started.

## How to support recovery

- **Keep moving, safely.** Gentle activity and physical therapy help prevent loss of strength. Clear the floor of rugs and clutter, and use good lighting at night.
- **Review the medicines.** Bring a complete list of medicines to every appointment, including over-the-counter ones and supplements, so pain medicine does not interact badly with other prescriptions.
- **Arrange transportation.** Many older adults stop going to appointments because they cannot or do not want to drive after the crash. Ask whether the clinic offers transportation.
- **Talk about driving again calmly.** Fear after a crash is common at any age: see [fear of driving after a car accident](/en/blog/fear-of-driving-after-car-accident).

## Who pays if they have Medicare

In Utah, the PIP of the auto policy pays the first medical bills, regardless of who caused the crash. Under Medicare's rules, auto insurance usually pays first and Medicare pays after. That is why it is important to tell every provider that the injury came from a car accident and give them the PIP claim number. Read more in [who pays medical bills after a car accident in Utah](/en/blog/who-pays-medical-bills-car-accident-utah).

## The short version

In older adults, a "minor" crash can cause fractures or bleeding that show up later. If they take blood thinners and hit their head, or seem more confused or drowsy, go to the ER. Watch them closely for the first two weeks, keep them moving safely, review their medicines and go with them to their appointments.
