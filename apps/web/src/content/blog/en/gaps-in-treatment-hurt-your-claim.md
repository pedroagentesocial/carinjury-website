Nobody plans a treatment gap. It happens like this: you start feeling better, you miss a Thursday because of work, then the following week the car is in the shop, then it is the holidays — and suddenly it has been five weeks since your last visit.

Six months later, that five-week stretch is the first thing the adjuster's letter mentions.

## What actually counts as a "gap"

A gap is any meaningful break in the chain of documented care related to your injury. Insurers look at four kinds:

<div class="pa-table-wrap">
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th>Type of gap</th><th>What it looks like</th><th>The argument it invites</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Initial delay</td><td>Crash on the 1st, first medical visit on the 20th</td><td>"If it hurt, you would have gone sooner — something else caused this"</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Mid-treatment gap</td><td>Weekly visits, then nothing for a month, then back</td><td>"You had recovered; the later care is unrelated"</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Early discharge</td><td>Stopping care while still symptomatic</td><td>"The injury resolved by that date"</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Ignored referral</td><td>An MRI or specialist consult recommended but never done</td><td>"Even your own provider's plan wasn't serious enough to follow"</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

## Why a calendar beats a narrative

Adjusters do not read your medical records as a story. They build a timeline and look for holes in it, because inconsistent care makes both **causation** ("did the crash cause this?") and **damages** ("how bad was it really?") easier to challenge at once.

Every week without documented care is a week they get to characterise for you.</p>

## The medical cost is the bigger one

Long before it becomes a legal problem, a treatment gap is a clinical problem.

<div class="pa-timeline">
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Weeks 0–2</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Inflammation phase</p>
    <p>Tissue is swollen and painful. Gentle, guided movement in this window prevents the guarding pattern that causes most long-term stiffness.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Weeks 2–6</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Repair phase — the decisive one</p>
    <p>New collagen is laid down. Whether it aligns properly or scars in a short, disorganised pattern depends almost entirely on controlled loading during these weeks. This is the worst possible time to disappear.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Weeks 6–12</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Remodelling phase</p>
    <p>Strength and endurance are rebuilt. Stopping here is why people say they "recovered but never got back to normal".</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Months 3+</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Chronicity risk</p>
    <p>Pain that persists past this point becomes harder to treat and more likely to involve central sensitisation, not just tissue damage.</p>
  </div>
</div>

Feeling better at week three is not evidence of healing being finished. It is usually evidence that inflammation has settled while the repair phase is only beginning.

## Gaps that are survivable — and how

A delay does not automatically end a claim. What kills it is an *unexplained* delay. The fix is to convert the gap from a hole in the record into a documented event.

<div class="pa-compare">
  <div class="pa-col pa-col-good">
    <h4>Defensible, if documented</h4>
    <ul>
      <li>No transportation — note it and use a clinic that provides rides</li>
      <li>Cost or exhausted PIP — put the financial reason in the chart</li>
      <li>Clinic had no availability — keep the scheduling record</li>
      <li>Flu, surgery, pregnancy, family emergency</li>
      <li>Work schedule change — ask for evening or weekend slots in writing</li>
      <li>Provider told you to pause and reassess on a set date</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-col pa-col-bad">
    <h4>Hard to defend</h4>
    <ul>
      <li>"I felt a bit better so I stopped"</li>
      <li>No-shows with no call and no note</li>
      <li>Gym or sports activity during the gap, visible on social media</li>
      <li>Returning only after speaking to a lawyer, with no symptom log in between</li>
      <li>Never following a recommended referral</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>If you already have a gap, do this now</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Go back. A gap that ended is far better than a gap that is still open.</li>
    <li>At the return visit, ask the provider to record why you were away and what symptoms persisted during the break.</li>
    <li>Gather proof of the reason: work schedules, repair invoices, other medical records, bus routes.</li>
    <li>Restart a daily symptom log immediately — the gap has no notes, so the present must.</li>
    <li>Tell your attorney about the gap before the adjuster does.</li>
    <li>Schedule the next four appointments at once, rather than one at a time.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## How to prevent the next one

Most gaps are logistics problems wearing a medical costume: no ride, wrong hours, unclear billing. Solve the logistics and the gap disappears.

## The short version

Your medical record is a timeline, and insurers read the empty spaces as loudly as the visits. Keep the chain unbroken while you are healing; if it does break, close it quickly and make sure the reason is written down by someone other than you.
