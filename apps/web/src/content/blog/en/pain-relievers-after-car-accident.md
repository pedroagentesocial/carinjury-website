The night after the crash your neck is stiff and your lower back aches, so you take a couple of ibuprofen. A week later you are still taking them every few hours, and you have added Tylenol "for the bad moments."

Over-the-counter pain relievers can help you get through the first days. But **they do not heal the injury, they can hide how bad it is, and taking them for weeks has real risks.** Here is what to know before you open the bottle.

MedlinePlus warns not to take more than **4,000 mg of acetaminophen a day from all sources**, and not to take more than one product containing acetaminophen at a time. That limit is easy to pass without noticing if you combine Tylenol with a "nighttime" cold medicine or a prescription pain pill that already contains it.

For NSAIDs, MedlinePlus notes that the risk of a heart attack or stroke can appear at any time during treatment and may be higher with long use or high doses, and that ulcers and bleeding can happen without warning symptoms. ## What the evidence says about pills for back pain

For acute low back pain — one of the most common injuries after a crash — the American College of Physicians recommends starting with **non-drug treatments**: superficial heat, massage, acupuncture or spinal manipulation, because most people improve over time regardless of treatment. If medication is wanted, the guideline recommends NSAIDs or muscle relaxants.

Acetaminophen did not do well in that setting. In a large randomized trial of 1,652 people with acute low back pain, the median time to recovery was 17 days with regular acetaminophen and 16 days with a placebo — no meaningful difference. That does not mean it never helps anyone, but it is a reason not to rely on it alone.

Read more in [heat, ultrasound and massage, or exercise?](/en/blog/passive-vs-active-care-after-car-accident) and [back pain after a car accident](/en/blog/back-pain-after-car-accident).

## Muscle relaxants and driving

If a doctor prescribes a muscle relaxant such as cyclobenzaprine, know that it can make you drowsy. MedlinePlus advises not to drive or operate machinery until you know how it affects you, and to ask your doctor about alcohol, because it can make its effects worse. MedlinePlus also says older adults usually should not take cyclobenzaprine, because other medicines are safer or work better for them.

## Masking the pain is not the same as treating it

- **It can hide a worsening injury.** Pain that is "under control" with pills every few hours is still pain. Tell your provider how much you are taking, not just how you feel on it.
- **It can delay care.** Some injuries show up over days. See [delayed symptoms after a car accident](/en/blog/delayed-symptoms-after-car-accident).
- **Frequent painkillers can cause headaches.** MedlinePlus warns that taking pain medicines more than 3 days a week may lead to rebound headaches. Read [headaches after a car accident](/en/blog/headaches-after-car-accident).
- **It does not restore movement.** Stiffness and weakness after a crash usually need active rehab. See [your first six weeks of rehab after a crash](/en/blog/first-six-weeks-physical-therapy-after-crash).

## The short version

Acetaminophen and NSAIDs like ibuprofen can make the first days after a crash more bearable, but they do not treat the injury. Stay under 4,000 mg of acetaminophen a day from all sources, ask before taking NSAIDs if you have heart, kidney or stomach problems, take blood thinners or are pregnant, and do not drive on a new muscle relaxant until you know how it affects you. If you are still reaching for pills after about 10 days, it is time for a real evaluation.
