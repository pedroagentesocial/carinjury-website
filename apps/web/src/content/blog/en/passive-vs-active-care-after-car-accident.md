Hot packs, ultrasound, electrical stimulation, massage. They feel good, and after a crash feeling good matters.

But there is a question few patients ask: **is this healing me, or only relieving me?** That is exactly the difference between passive and active care, and understanding it can take weeks off your recovery.

## Passive vs active in one sentence

- **Passive care** is everything applied to you while you lie on a table: heat, ice, ultrasound, TENS or electrical stimulation, massage.
- **Active care** is everything you do: range-of-motion exercises, stretching, strengthening, gradually returning to your activities. Manual therapy — adjustments, mobilizations — belongs here too when it is combined with exercise.

Both have a place. The problem is when a plan never moves past the first one.

## What the clinical guidelines say

The most cited guideline for neck pain and whiplash from traffic collisions comes from the **OPTIMa** group (Ontario Protocol for Traffic Injury Management), published in the *European Spine Journal* in 2016. For recent mild to moderate injuries (0 to 3 months), it recommends considering:

- **Gentle range-of-motion exercises** at home, taught by a professional: 5 to 10 repetitions without resistance, several times a day.
- **Structured education** about the injury — combined with exercise, never on its own.
- **Multimodal care**: range-of-motion exercise together with manipulation or mobilization.

And it advises **against offering as treatments on their own** a cervical collar, clinic-based heat, electrotherapy or relaxation massage, because they did not show a real benefit on recovery.

The same guideline reminds us of two things worth hearing: most patients recover, and no treatment is magic — the measured benefits are modest. What matters most is staying in motion.

## What each therapy is actually for

  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th>Therapy</th><th>Type</th><th>What it is really for</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td>Ice</td><td>Passive</td><td>Reducing swelling and pain in the first 48–72 hours</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Heat</td><td>Passive</td><td>Relaxing tight muscle before you move; useful at home as a warm-up</td></tr>
      <tr><td>TENS / e-stim</td><td>Passive</td><td>Temporary pain relief so you can do your exercises</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Therapeutic ultrasound</td><td>Passive</td><td>An add-on for tissue comfort; no strong evidence as a stand-alone treatment</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Massage</td><td>Passive</td><td>Easing muscle tension; more useful for lingering pain, alongside exercise</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Adjustments and mobilizations</td><td>Manual</td><td>Restoring joint motion, especially when combined with exercise</td></tr>
      <tr><td>Range-of-motion and strengthening exercise</td><td>Active</td><td>Rebuilding motion, strength and confidence — the core of the plan</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

The right way to think about it: **passive care opens the door, active care walks you through it.** If heat and TENS lower your pain enough to do your exercises, they are doing their job. If they are all you get week after week, something is missing.

## What a good plan should look like

## The short version

Heat, ultrasound, TENS and massage relieve pain and help you move, but they are not the main treatment. What has the most support after whiplash is staying active with guided exercise, combined with manual therapy. A good plan starts with more relief and ends with you doing most of the work.
