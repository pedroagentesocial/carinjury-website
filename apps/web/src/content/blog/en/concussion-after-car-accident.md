Two beliefs keep people from getting a concussion diagnosed after a crash:

1. *"I never hit my head."*
2. *"I never lost consciousness."*

Neither is required. A concussion is caused by the brain moving rapidly inside the skull — and a collision that whips your head forward and back does exactly that, with or without contact. Most concussions involve **no loss of consciousness at all**.

## The four symptom groups the CDC uses

Concussion symptoms are easy to dismiss one by one. Grouped, the pattern is much harder to miss.

<div class="pa-table-wrap">
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr><th>Group</th><th>What it looks like day to day</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td><strong>Thinking / remembering</strong></td><td>Feeling slowed down or "in a fog", trouble concentrating, forgetting new information, rereading the same paragraph</td></tr>
      <tr><td><strong>Physical</strong></td><td>Headache, blurry vision, dizziness, nausea, balance problems, sensitivity to light or noise, ringing in the ears, fatigue</td></tr>
      <tr><td><strong>Emotional / mood</strong></td><td>Irritability, sadness, anxiety, feeling unusually emotional or short-tempered</td></tr>
      <tr><td><strong>Sleep</strong></td><td>Sleeping much more or much less than usual, trouble falling asleep, waking unrefreshed</td></tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

The CDC also notes that symptoms **change during recovery**: headaches and nausea tend to come first, while emotional symptoms and sleep problems often surface a week or two later. Patients frequently report those later symptoms to no one, because by then they no longer connect them to the crash.

  <span class="pa-note-icon">🚨</span>
  <div>
    <p><strong>Emergency danger signs — call 911 or go to an ER now:</strong> one pupil larger than the other · drowsiness or inability to wake up · a headache that keeps getting worse · slurred speech, weakness, numbness or decreased coordination · repeated vomiting or nausea · convulsions or seizures · unusual behaviour, increased confusion, restlessness or agitation · loss of consciousness, even briefly.</p>

## "But the CT scan was normal"

This is the most common source of confusion, and it is worth being precise about.

<div class="pa-compare">
  <div class="pa-col pa-col-good">
    <h4>What a CT scan is for</h4>
    <ul>
      <li>Ruling out bleeding inside the skull</li>
      <li>Ruling out a skull fracture</li>
      <li>Deciding whether you need emergency neurosurgery</li>
      <li>It is a safety test — and a normal result is good news</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-col pa-col-bad">
    <h4>What a CT scan cannot do</h4>
    <ul>
      <li>Rule out a concussion</li>
      <li>Show microscopic stretching of nerve fibres</li>
      <li>Measure balance, eye tracking or reaction time</li>
      <li>Tell you when it is safe to return to work or driving</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

A concussion is a **functional** injury, diagnosed clinically — by history, symptom inventory, cognitive screening, balance testing and eye-movement assessment. A normal scan rules out the emergency; it does not rule out the injury.

## What recovery actually looks like

<div class="pa-timeline">
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Days 1–3</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Relative rest — not a dark room</p>
    <p>Reduce, don't eliminate. Cut back screens, loud environments and intense exercise, but stay lightly active. Prolonged total rest is no longer recommended and tends to prolong symptoms.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Days 3–10</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Gradual reintroduction</p>
    <p>Light walking, short work blocks with breaks, controlled screen time. The rule of thumb: activity that mildly bumps symptoms is acceptable; activity that spikes them is too much, too soon.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Weeks 2–4</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Most people are back to normal</p>
    <p>The CDC notes most people return to work, school and usual activities within days to weeks. Recovery is slower for older adults, young children, and anyone with a previous concussion.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Beyond 4 weeks</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Persistent symptoms need a plan</p>
    <p>Symptoms lasting months may be discussed as post-concussive syndrome. At this point, treating the neck matters: cervical injury and concussion produce overlapping headaches and dizziness, and the neck component is treatable.</p>
  </div>
</div>

## The neck-and-brain overlap almost nobody addresses

The same forces that concuss the brain sprain the cervical spine. That is why so many post-crash patients have both — and why treating only one leaves symptoms behind.

Dizziness, headache at the base of the skull, light sensitivity and difficulty concentrating can all come from the neck as much as the head. A good evaluation checks both. If your headaches are the dominant complaint, our guide to [headaches after a car accident](/en/blog/headaches-after-car-accident) breaks the types apart, and [what is whiplash](/en/blog/what-is-whiplash) covers the cervical side.

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>If you suspect a concussion after a crash</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Get evaluated within 24–72 hours, even with a normal ER visit and a normal CT.</li>
    <li>Do not drive while you have dizziness, delayed reactions or vision problems.</li>
    <li>Avoid a second head impact until you are cleared — a repeat concussion during recovery is far more serious.</li>
    <li>Ask someone at home to watch for the emergency danger signs for the first 48 hours.</li>
    <li>Track symptoms daily on a 0–10 scale; changes over time matter more than any single day.</li>
    <li>Be careful with alcohol and sedatives while symptomatic.</li>
    <li>Tell your employer or school you may need temporary accommodations — it is normal and usually short-lived.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## The short version

You do not need a blow to the head, a lost minute of memory, or an abnormal scan to have a concussion. What you need is someone to actually test for one. Most people recover in days to weeks with early, guided care — and the ones who struggle longest are usually the ones who were told "the scan was fine" and sent home without a plan.
