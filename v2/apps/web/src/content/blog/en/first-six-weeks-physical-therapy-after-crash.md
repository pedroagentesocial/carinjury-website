The most common question in week one is "how long until I'm normal again?" The most useful question is "what should be happening this week?"

Recovery from a crash injury is not one long wait. It is a sequence, and each phase has a job. When patients understand the sequence, they stop panicking in week two and stop quitting in week four.

## First, which grade are you?

The Quebec Task Force classification of whiplash-associated disorders (WAD) is still the standard shorthand clinicians use, and it drives how aggressive the early plan should be.

      <tr><td><strong>0</strong></td><td>No complaint, no physical signs</td><td>Monitoring only</td></tr>

## The plan, week by week

<div class="pa-timeline">
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Week 1 · Protect and calm</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Reduce pain, keep moving</p>
    <p>Gentle range-of-motion within tolerance, ice or heat, soft-tissue work, education. The goal is not to be pain-free — it is to prevent the guarding pattern that makes week four hard. Collars, if used at all, come off within the first 72 hours.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Week 2 · Restore motion</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Range of motion becomes the target</p>
    <p>Manual therapy and mobilisation, controlled active movement in every direction, posture and workstation adjustments. Measured neck rotation should start improving in degrees, not feelings.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Weeks 3–4 · Activate</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Deep stabilisers wake up</p>
    <p>Low-load endurance work for the deep neck flexors, scapular control, hip and core stability. Unglamorous, slightly boring, and the single best predictor of not relapsing at month three.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Weeks 5–6 · Load and integrate</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Back to real life</p>
    <p>Progressive strengthening, longer sitting and driving tolerance, lifting mechanics, return-to-work tasks. Symptoms during activity are expected to settle within 24 hours; if they don't, the load was too high.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Beyond week 6</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Reassess honestly</p>
    <p>If objective measures have plateaued, the plan changes — imaging correlated with the exam, a specialist opinion, or a different treatment approach. Repeating an unchanged plan for another six weeks is not persistence.</p>
  </div>
</div>

## Why "rest until it stops hurting" fails

The Quebec Task Force recommendations pushed the field in a specific direction: **emphasise early return to usual activity and promote mobility**, using manual treatment to relieve pain and enable movement. The same body of work found that prolonged use of a soft cervical collar beyond the first 72 hours probably prolongs disability.

The mechanism is straightforward. Immobilised tissue heals shorter and stiffer, muscles deconditioned in three weeks take considerably longer than three weeks to rebuild, and the nervous system learns to guard a region it perceives as fragile. Movement, dosed correctly, is the input that tells the repair process how to organise itself.

<div class="pa-note">
  <span class="pa-note-icon">📏</span>
  <div>
    <p><strong>Ask to see your numbers.</strong> "How are you feeling?" is a poor progress measure — mood, sleep and weather all move it. Ask your provider for the objective ones: degrees of cervical rotation and extension, grip or muscle strength grades, a functional score, tolerance for sitting and driving. Numbers that improve are also the numbers that make a medical record persuasive later.</p>

## The four mistakes that stall week four

<div class="pa-compare">
  <div class="pa-col pa-col-bad">
    <h4>What derails recovery</h4>
    <ul>
      <li>Stopping when the pain eases but strength has not returned</li>
      <li>Doing only passive treatment — heat, ultrasound, massage — with no exercise</li>
      <li>Going back to the gym at full load in week two</li>
      <li>Skipping the home program and relying on two clinic visits a week</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-col pa-col-good">
    <h4>What accelerates it</h4>
    <ul>
      <li>Daily short sessions at home, even 10 minutes</li>
      <li>Sleeping enough — tissue repair is largely nocturnal</li>
      <li>Walking every day from week one</li>
      <li>Reporting new radiating symptoms immediately instead of at the next visit</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>Your six-week checklist</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Know your WAD grade and ask what it means for your plan.</li>
    <li>Get baseline measurements at visit one — you cannot show progress without a starting point.</li>
    <li>Do the home exercises daily; the clinic visit is coaching, not the treatment.</li>
    <li>Keep a 0–10 symptom log with what activity triggered what.</li>
    <li>Do not stop treatment early because you had three good days.</li>
    <li>Ask for a written reassessment at week six, with numbers.</li>
    <li>Report any new weakness, numbness or radiating pain the day it appears.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## The short version

The first six weeks are not a waiting room. They are the window in which healing tissue is most responsive to how you use it. Move early, load progressively, measure objectively, and do not confuse a good week with a finished recovery.

If your symptoms are mostly neck pain and headaches, start with [what is whiplash](/en/blog/what-is-whiplash); if they travel into an arm or leg, [herniated disc and sciatica after a car accident](/en/blog/herniated-disc-sciatica-after-car-accident) covers what changes.
