"I've had a headache since the accident" is one of the most common sentences we hear — and one of the least specific.

Post-traumatic headache (PTH) is an umbrella term covering several distinct patterns with different mechanisms. That distinction is not academic: it decides whether the right treatment is manual care of the upper neck, a nerve-focused approach, migraine management, or a combination. It is also why a month of over-the-counter painkillers so often does nothing.

## The five patterns

      <tr><th>Type</th><th>Where and how it feels</th><th>Typical clues</th></tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr><td><strong>Cervicogenic</strong></td><td>Starts at the base of the skull, spreads up behind the eye or into the forehead — usually one-sided</td><td>Provoked by neck position or movement; reduced neck rotation; tender upper cervical joints</td></tr>
      <tr><td><strong>Occipital neuralgia</strong></td><td>Sharp, electric, shooting pain across the scalp from the back of the head</td><td>Stabbing bursts, scalp tenderness or numbness, sometimes triggered by brushing hair</td></tr>

Mixed patterns are the norm, not the exception. A common combination after a rear-end collision is a cervicogenic base with superimposed migraine attacks — which is precisely why treating only one of them leaves the patient half-better and discouraged.

## Why the neck causes headaches at all

The upper three cervical nerve roots and the trigeminal nerve — the main sensory nerve of the face and head — share processing in the same region of the brainstem. Pain input from injured joints, discs and ligaments in the upper neck can therefore be *perceived* in the forehead, behind the eye or across the temple.

That convergence is the reason a patient who never hit their head can develop daily headaches after a crash, and the reason restoring upper cervical mobility often reduces headaches that painkillers did not touch.

## What raises the risk of headaches lasting

Research on post-traumatic headache identifies fairly consistent risk factors: a prior history of migraine or frequent headaches, being younger, being female, greater severity of the head injury, and co-occurring anxiety or depression. None of these are things you control — but knowing them changes how early and how actively a case should be managed rather than watched.

## What effective treatment looks like

<div class="pa-timeline">
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Step 1</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Classify before treating</p>
    <p>History (onset, location, triggers, duration), cervical examination, neurological screen, and a headache diary. The diary is unglamorous and it is often the piece that identifies the pattern.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Step 2</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Treat the cervical driver</p>
    <p>Manual therapy and mobilisation of the upper cervical segments, deep neck flexor training, soft-tissue work on the suboccipital muscles, posture and workstation correction. This is where cervicogenic headache responds.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Step 3</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Address the migraine component</p>
    <p>Trigger identification, sleep and hydration, and — when indicated — medical management. Watch for medication-overuse headache, a common trap when painkillers are taken most days.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Step 4</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Reassess at 4–6 weeks</p>
    <p>Frequency, intensity and duration should all be trending down. If they are not, the classification may be wrong or a specialist referral is due.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>Track these — it changes your treatment and your record</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Days per week with headache, not just "often".</li>
    <li>Peak intensity 0–10 and how long each episode lasts.</li>
    <li>Exact location: base of skull, one side, behind the eye, band around the head.</li>
    <li>Triggers: screen time, driving, neck position, sleep, noise, light.</li>
    <li>What you took, how much and whether it helped — including days with no medication.</li>
    <li>What you could not do because of it: work missed, driving avoided, plans cancelled.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## The short version

"Headache after a crash" is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Most post-traumatic headaches have a treatable cervical component, many have a migraine component, and a few are neuralgic — and each responds to something different. Classify it properly, treat the neck seriously, track it in numbers, and reassess at six weeks instead of waiting it out.
