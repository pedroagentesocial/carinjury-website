Two patients walk into the clinic the same week. One is convinced she needs an MRI immediately. The other was told his X-ray was clean and assumes he is fine.

Both are wrong, and for the same reason: **each imaging study answers one specific question**, and none of them answers "am I injured?"

<div class="pa-stats">
  <div class="pa-stat"><span class="pa-stat-num">X-ray</span><span class="pa-stat-label">Bone: fractures, alignment, arthritis, instability</span></div>
  <div class="pa-stat"><span class="pa-stat-num">CT</span><span class="pa-stat-label">Bone in detail + bleeding — the emergency test</span></div>
  <div class="pa-stat"><span class="pa-stat-num">MRI</span><span class="pa-stat-label">Discs, nerves, ligaments, muscle — soft tissue</span></div>

## How clinicians decide whether you need a neck scan at all

Emergency departments do not scan every crash patient. They use validated decision rules — mainly **NEXUS** and the **Canadian C-Spine Rule** — designed to catch clinically important cervical injuries while avoiding unnecessary radiation.

<div class="pa-compare">
  <div class="pa-col pa-col-good">
    <h4>NEXUS: imaging can be skipped if ALL are true</h4>
    <ul>
      <li>No midline cervical tenderness</li>
      <li>No focal neurologic deficit</li>
      <li>Normal alertness</li>
      <li>No intoxication</li>
      <li>No painful distracting injury</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-col pa-col-bad">
    <h4>Canadian C-Spine: imaging is required if ANY is true</h4>
    <ul>
      <li>Age 65 or older</li>
      <li>Dangerous mechanism of injury</li>
      <li>Paresthesias (tingling/numbness) in the extremities</li>
      <li>(Applied only to alert patients with GCS 15)</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

When one of those rules says imaging is needed, the American College of Radiology recommends **CT as the initial study for adults**, because it is more sensitive and specific than plain radiography for cervical trauma. In children, plain X-ray is generally performed first, with CT reserved for abnormal findings.

## When an MRI is genuinely worth ordering

MRIs are expensive and they find incidental, age-related changes in most adults. Ordered without a clinical reason, a "positive" MRI can even confuse a case. Ordered for the right reason, it changes the treatment plan.

<div class="pa-timeline">
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Immediately</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Red flags</p>
    <p>Progressive weakness, loss of bowel or bladder control, saddle numbness, or signs of spinal cord involvement. This is urgent, not a scheduling conversation.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Weeks 1–4</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Radiating symptoms that follow a nerve</p>
    <p>Pain, numbness or weakness travelling down one arm or one leg in a specific pattern — especially with a matching reflex or strength change on exam.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Weeks 4–6</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Failure to progress</p>
    <p>You have done consistent conservative care and objective measures — range of motion, strength, function — have plateaued or worsened.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="pa-step">
    <span class="pa-step-when">Before escalation</span>
    <p class="pa-step-title">Deciding on injections or surgery</p>
    <p>Any decision about epidural injections or a surgical consult needs imaging that matches the physical exam.</p>
  </div>
</div>

## The documentation angle

Imaging also plays a role in your claim, and it is worth understanding without letting it drive the medicine.

Objective findings — a documented fracture, a disc herniation matching your symptoms, measured loss of range of motion — are the evidence that separates a well-supported claim from a disputed one. But an MRI ordered "for the file" and unconnected to your exam is not persuasive; adjusters and their reviewers are very good at spotting degenerative findings that predate the crash. The strongest record is a clinical exam that changes over time, supported by imaging obtained for a stated clinical reason. That thread is exactly what we cover in [how to document your injuries for an insurance claim](/en/blog/how-to-document-injuries-insurance-claim).

<div class="pa-check">
  <h4>Questions worth asking before any scan</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>What specific question is this study answering?</li>
    <li>Would the result change my treatment plan?</li>
    <li>Is there a less expensive or lower-radiation option that answers the same question?</li>
    <li>Will my PIP or health insurance cover it, and does the clinic bill directly?</li>
    <li>Who will interpret it alongside my physical exam — not in isolation?</li>
  </ul>
</div>

## The short version

X-ray rules out fractures. CT is the emergency test for bone and bleeding. MRI is for soft tissue and nerves, and is most useful after a few weeks of persistent or radiating symptoms. None of them can see a sprain, and none of them can see pain — which is why the physical exam, repeated over time, remains the centre of the diagnosis.
